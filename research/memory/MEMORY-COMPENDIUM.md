**The book in three theses** (argued in full inside; stated here so a busy reader can test them against every chapter):

1. **Decode is a streaming problem, not a computing problem.** Since Wulf & McKee (1995), the gap between compute growth and memory growth has made memory the binding constraint of the era; LLM decode, at ~1 FLOP per byte at batch 1, is its purest expression. Almost every important runner decision is a decision about bytes: how many, where they live, and how often they move. (Chapters 1, 4, 8.)
2. **Every level of the hierarchy is managed by the same four verbs — allocate, place, evict, account — and the winning design at each level is the one that refuses generality the workload does not need.** Paged KV pools are slab allocators; prefix caches are content-addressed stores; agent memory is a database with an editor. Fragmentation theory, allocator lineage, and serving practice are one subject seen at three altitudes. (Chapters 2, 3, 5.)
3. **Honesty is a memory property, purchased at write and manage time.** A cache that silently truncates, a store that appends without superseding, and a benchmark that reports speed without bytes all fail the same way: they preserve a fluent surface over a corrupt record. The systems that can be trusted — in this corpus, measurably — are the ones that version, disclose, and grade against an oracle. (Chapters 7, 9.)

## Chapter 1 — The Wall: Memory as the Binding Constraint of the Computing Era

### 1.1 The original argument, and why it has not expired

Wm. A. Wulf and Sally A. McKee, "Hitting the Memory Wall" (ACM SIGARCH Computer Architecture News, March 1995; note dated December 1994), made an argument about diverging exponentials, and it is worth restating exactly because later folklore blurred it (full treatment: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md` §1).

Microprocessor performance was improving at roughly 80% per year (Baskett's 1991 estimate, as they cite it); DRAM performance at roughly 7% per year (Hennessy & Patterson, 1990). Each grows exponentially, but *the difference between diverging exponentials also grows exponentially*. Wulf & McKee made it concrete with average memory access time, `t_avg = p·t_c + (1−p)·t_m`, under assumptions deliberately generous to the optimist: cache speed scales with the processor, the cache is *perfect* (only compulsory misses, at 1% or less), and the machine is already using the full bandwidth of memory. Even so, the average cost per access becomes 1.52 cycles in 2000, 8.25 cycles in 2005, and 98.8 cycles in 2010. They define the wall as the point where `t_avg` exceeds five instruction times — at that point "system performance is totally determined by memory speed; making the processor faster won't affect the wall-clock time." Changing the hit rate (even to 99.8%) or the miss-cost ratio moves the date, not the outcome. And the escape hatches fail by construction: "We have already assumed a perfect cache, so a bigger/smarter one won't help. We're already using the full bandwidth of the memory, so prefetching or other related schemes won't help either." Every known technique, they note, provides a one-time boost; none changes the fundamentals.

Two precisions the corpus insists on keeping. First, the 1995 note is a *latency*-framed argument — average access time — not yet a bandwidth argument; conflating the two was the flaw McCalpin later corrected (§1.2). Second, it is honestly a trend argument: "Our prediction of the memory wall is probably wrong too." What survives is not any year's number but the structural claim: when two exponentials diverge, the slower one eventually sets the system's speed. Thirty years on, that claim is the load-bearing wall of this entire book.

### 1.2 McCalpin: measure the delivered side, and keep latency apart from bandwidth

John D. McCalpin supplied the measurement culture the wall argument lacked (`memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md` §1.3). Three contributions:

- **Machine balance** — the ratio of computation offered to memory traffic sustainable — measured as *sustainable* bandwidth: "the sustainable data transfer rates for uncached unit-stride vector operations," not vendor peaks.
- **STREAM** (introduced 1991) — four long-vector operations (Copy, Scale, Add, Triad) reporting the sustained rate ordinary programs can achieve. Its historical effect, in McCalpin's own SC16 summary, was to get "vendors to pay attention to sustained, rather than peak, bandwidths." Its ~1,100 contributed results document the changing balance over 25 years.
- **The critique.** The 1994/95 note "did not distinguish latency & bandwidth — this is crucial to understanding 'the wall' for real applications." A workload can be latency-bound (dependent, unpredictable accesses; too little concurrency in flight) while using little bandwidth, or bandwidth-bound (streaming, predictable, fully pipelined) while insensitive to latency. They need different fixes. McCalpin's figures make the concurrency point quantitative: DRAM latency in CPU clocks grew from ~14 clocks (1991) to ~400 clocks (2003), so sustaining even a fixed fraction of peak bandwidth requires an order of magnitude more concurrent references in flight. Concurrency is part of the bandwidth story, not a separate topic.

Sally McKee's retrospective ("Reflections on the Memory Wall," CF'04) asked whether we hit it and answered: **yes, for many types of applications** — with the workload-dependence doing real work. On the STREAM record, peak MFLOPS had grown at ~50%/yr against sustained bandwidth at ~35%/yr (and only ~15%/yr for the highest-performing machine). The gap in *measured* numbers is smaller than the 1995 framing, points the same way, and is a gap in delivered numbers. The interesting question thereby became: *for which workloads, which level of the hierarchy, and which resource — latency or bandwidth — binds first?* That is the question the roofline model answers per kernel.

### 1.3 The roofline method: the working instrument

Williams, Waterman & Patterson, "Roofline" (Communications of the ACM, April 2009), turned the wall into a per-kernel method (`memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md` §2):

- **Operational intensity** = operations per byte of DRAM traffic — bytes counted *between the caches and memory*, after the hierarchy has filtered what it can. Intensity is therefore a property of a kernel *as run on a hierarchy*: better locality raises it.
- **The bound:** Attainable performance = min(Peak Floating-Point Performance, Peak Memory Bandwidth × Operational Intensity). Plotted log-log, the bandwidth term is a 45° slope and the compute term a flat ceiling — a roof. The **ridge point** is the intensity where they meet. Left of it, a kernel is memory-bound: go faster only by using bandwidth better or moving fewer bytes. Right of it, compute-bound: more bandwidth buys nothing.
- The model is an **upper bound, not a prediction**. Distance below the roof is diagnostic information. In its 2009 form it models one memory level and one compute ceiling; later practice adds per-level roofs, but the discipline is unchanged: name the resource, measure its delivered peak, compute the intensity, locate the kernel.
- It says nothing by itself about **capacity** (whether the working set fits at all) or about latency-bound small kernels. Those need their own measurements — a distinction that matters directly for small-GPU inference (Chapter 9).

The 2009 worked example (2.2 GHz AMD Opteron X2, 17.6 GFLOP/s peak, intensities 0.25–16 FLOP/byte) is a useful fossil: the ridge sat at single-digit FLOP/byte. On an H100 SXM, by the same formula from vendor peaks reported in the corpus's sources (~989 TFLOP/s BF16 ÷ 3.35 TB/s), the ridge is ~295 FLOP/byte. The roof has risen two orders of magnitude. Almost nothing real lives to the right of it any more.

### 1.4 The LLM-era restatement: prefill computes, decode streams

Yuan et al., "LLM Inference Unveiled" (arXiv:2402.16363, 2024), applied the roofline to LLM inference systematically (`memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md` §3). Its measured centrepiece — LLaMA-2-7B on an NVIDIA A6000, Table 1 values as reported via secondary coverage read for the dossier and flagged as such there — is that *the same layer has opposite character in the two phases*: the query projection measures ~1,024 OPs/byte in prefill (compute-bound) versus ~1 OP/byte in decode (memory-bound); the feed-forward gate projection ~1,215 versus ~1. Its conclusion for decode: "in the decode stage, all computations are memory-bound."

The phase split follows from shapes, and the derivation is short enough to carry in your head:

- **Prefill** processes P prompt tokens in parallel. Each weight matrix is read once and used for all P positions — a matrix–matrix product (GEMM). FLOPs per weight byte scale with P; intensity is hundreds to thousands of FLOP/byte. **Prefill is compute-bound.** Its user-visible metric is time-to-first-token (TTFT).
- **Decode** generates one token per step per sequence. The same matrices are multiplied by a single vector (GEMV). Each weight is read, used for ~one multiply-add, discarded: ~2 FLOPs per parameter against 2 bytes per FP16 parameter ≈ **1 FLOP/byte at batch 1** — two-plus orders of magnitude left of an H100-class ridge. **Decode is bandwidth-bound**, with the closed-form ceiling:

> tokens/sec per sequence ≲ sustained bandwidth ÷ bytes read per token,
> where bytes-per-token ≈ active weight bytes + KV-cache bytes read that step.

Worked example from the corpus's sources: a 70B model in FP16 (~140 GB of weights) on a 3.35 TB/s H100 tops out near ~20 tokens/sec per sequence — not for lack of FLOPs, but because 140 GB must stream through the chip for every token.

- **The KV cache is part of the per-token traffic, and it grows.** Every decode step reads the keys and values of all previous tokens. KV bytes per token are fixed by architecture (Chapter 3), so the KV term is small at short context and increasingly material at long context — taxing, as one corpus source puts it, "the phase that is already starved for bandwidth." Long-context decode is *more* bandwidth-bound, not less; its ceiling falls as the conversation grows even with weights unchanged.

**Batching** is the one lever that moves decode's intensity itself, which is why it dominates serving-system design. With B sequences decoded together, weights are read once per step and used for B tokens: intensity ≈ B (in the FP16 form), and aggregate throughput climbs near-linearly in B while decode remains on the bandwidth slope. This continues until the ridge is reached (B on the order of ~150–300 for H100-class parts in the reports read), KV *capacity* runs out, or latency SLOs forbid larger batches. Continuous (iteration-level) batching — introduced by Orca (`papers/orca.md`) and now standard in vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, and SGLang — exists to keep B high in practice; chunked prefill and prefill/decode disaggregation (Sarathi-Serve, DistServe, Splitwise — `papers/sarathi-serve.md`, `papers/distserve.md`, `papers/splitwise.md`) exist because mixing a compute-bound prefill into a decode batch lengthens every decode step in it.

What batching does *not* change: per-sequence latency at batch 1, the bytes-per-token floor for a single stream, or prefill's character. A single-user local workload lives at B ≈ 1 by definition. For that regime the remaining levers are the ones that cut bytes: **quantization** (fewer bytes per weight → proportionally higher decode ceiling on the same bandwidth — why Q4-class formats dominate local inference), keeping weights resident in the fastest memory that fits them, and reducing KV bytes (fewer KV heads, KV quantization, shorter effective context). **[Synthesis judgement]** This asymmetry — fleets buy intensity with batching, individuals buy it with compression — is the single most important economic fact in local inference, and most cross-audience advice fails by ignoring it.

### 1.5 Latency, one more time

McCalpin's distinction survives intact and explains a persistent confusion: at very small scale (tiny models, small GPUs, short contexts), a decode step's time can be dominated by fixed costs — kernel launches, synchronisation, host-side scheduling — rather than by streaming bytes. Such a step is latency/overhead-bound, sits *below* the bandwidth slope, and will not speed up proportionally with quantization. The roofline tells you this too — but only if you plot the *measured* point instead of assuming it lies on the slope. (The roofline dossier labels this paragraph, in its own text, as synthesis applied to the small-model regime; this volume carries the label forward.)

**Chapter thesis.** The wall is not a metaphor and not a date. It is a ratio that worsened for three decades and then met a workload — autoregressive decode — engineered, as if deliberately, to sit at its worst point. Everything after this chapter is tactics against that fact.

## Chapter 2 — The Hierarchy as It Is

### 2.1 The ladder, in orders of magnitude

A modern inference machine is not a processor with memory attached. It is a stack of memories with processors interleaved, and every design decision in this book is a decision about which level holds which bytes. The corpus documents the ladder most concretely at its GPU end (FlashAttention dossier, `papers/flashattention.md` §2): an A100 carries 40–80 GB of HBM at 1.5–2.0 TB/s, against 192 KB of on-chip SRAM per streaming multiprocessor at an estimated ~19 TB/s — SRAM roughly an order of magnitude faster and many orders of magnitude smaller. Generalising with the corpus's other anchors:

| Level | Typical scale (as documented in the corpus) | Governing fact |
|---|---|---|
| Registers | bytes per thread; register pressure sets occupancy (`memory/00-MEMORY-FIELD-MAP.md` C3) | The only level with no address; spilling is a cliff, not a slope |
| On-chip SRAM (shared memory / LDS, L1/L2/LLC) | 192 KB per SM (A100); tens of MB of LLC on CPUs; Strix Halo per-CCD L3 32 MiB (MimirAlloc Z13 records) | Where tiling lives; FlashAttention's whole method is refusing to leave it (Chapter 4) |
| HBM / GDDR (discrete VRAM) | 6 GB on our fleet's RTX 2060 Max-Q (purplelongship, verified at driver install 2026-10-28); 40–80 GB A100; ~3.35 TB/s H100 | The discrete budget: hard ceiling, local failure (Chapter 3, §7.2 of the KV dossier) |
| LPDDR (unified pools) | 128 GB on Strix Halo at 256 GB/s theoretical, ~215 GB/s reported sustained; Apple M4 Max 128 GB at 546 GB/s published (`memory/unified-memory.md` §2) | Capacity abundant, bandwidth rationed, failure global (Chapter 6) |
| Host DRAM (discrete systems) | The "forgiving overflow tier" of discrete design — and, on UMA, the same pool counted twice by careless accounting | PCIe spill collapses effective bandwidth for spilled bytes to interconnect rates (roofline dossier §4.1) |
| NVMe / SSD tiers | CacheBlend's testbed measured 4.8 GB/s on 1 TB NVMe (`papers/cacheblend.md`); SGLang HiCache L3 backends treat disk as the cache's bottom tier | Three orders below HBM; viable only as a *reuse* tier (restore beats recompute), never as a working tier |

Latency spans the same ladder in the other direction: the MimirAlloc Z13 campaign measured intra-CCD latency ~32 ns against cross-CCD ~103 ns (3.2×) on one package (`memory/mimiralloc-our-own-proven-work.md` §4.3) — a reminder that "one pool" never means "uniform cost," even before leaving the chip. McCalpin's historical figures (DRAM latency ~14 CPU clocks in 1991, ~400 in 2003) state the long trend: every level's latency, in the currency of the level above it, keeps rising.

> **Corpus gap, flagged here and owned in Chapter 10.** The field map's B-side — virtual memory and paging (B1), cache hierarchies and TLBs (B2), NUMA topology (B3), DRAM generations (B6), CXL pooling (B8) — has no dedicated banked dossier. What this chapter says about those levels is assembled from the dossiers that touch them in passing (allocator lineages on page management, unified memory on TTM/GTT, MimirAlloc on CCD topology). It is correct as far as it goes and thinner than the A/E sides. A reader who needs B-side depth today should treat this chapter as a map of where the corpus is shallow, not as coverage.

### 2.2 The four verbs

**[Synthesis judgement — the chapter's organising claim.]** Every level of the ladder, from register file to SSD tier to agent store, is managed by the same four operations, and designs differ mainly in how honestly and how cheaply they perform them:

1. **Allocate** — obtain units from the level's pool. Contiguous arena, paged blocks, slab objects, mmap extents, database rows: the shapes differ, the verb does not (Chapters 3, 5).
2. **Place** — decide *where* in the level (and at which level) a unit lives: per-CCD placement on Strix Halo, hot/cold neuron placement in PowerInfer (`runners/powerinfer.md`), layer-first versus page-first layout across HiCache tiers (`runners/sglang.md`), pinned versus pageable on UMA.
3. **Evict** — decide what leaves when the level fills, and what leaving costs: LRU over unreferenced prefix-tree leaves, heavy-hitter scoring over KV, decay-based purging in jemalloc, tombstoned supersession in an agent store. Eviction is where every level's honesty is tested, because eviction is where silent loss happens.
4. **Account** — know, at any moment, what is live, what is held, what is free, and in what size pieces. PyTorch's OOM message (allocated vs reserved vs reserved-unallocated) and jemalloc's `mallctl` decomposition are the corpus's exemplars of accounting as a feature; Ollama's silent truncation is its exemplar of accounting absent (Chapter 9).

The verbs compose across levels. A KV block is *allocated* from a GPU pool that was itself *allocated* from a driver segment that was *placed* by TTM inside a GTT budget that an administrator *accounted* — or failed to account — against the whole machine (Chapter 6). A failure at any level's accounting propagates upward as a mystery at every level above it. This is why the book treats allocation (Chapter 5) not as a library topic but as the discipline that the whole hierarchy shares.

### 2.3 Two currencies, never one

Little's-law reasoning and McCalpin's distinction (§1.2) give the hierarchy two independent currencies — **latency** (how long one access takes) and **bandwidth** (how many bytes per second the level sustains under concurrency) — plus a third quantity that is not a currency but a gate: **capacity** (whether the working set fits at all). The roofline dossier's discipline for a 6 GB GPU states the ordering plainly: "Capacity is the first wall on 6 GB, bandwidth the second." On a 128 GB UMA machine the order inverts: capacity is abundant, bandwidth binds first, and aggregate capacity returns as a *safety* problem rather than a fit problem (Chapter 6). **[Synthesis judgement]** Most memory arguments in engineering practice are currency confusions: a latency fix proposed for a bandwidth problem, a capacity purchase proposed for a latency problem, a benchmark that reports one currency while claiming another. Naming the currency is half the method; Chapter 9 is the other half.

### 2.4 Design law of the chapter

Because each level is ~10× smaller and ~10× faster than the one below it (in bandwidth terms, roughly, at the levels the corpus measures), *traffic between levels dominates everything*. The corollary that organises Chapters 3–4: the art of memory engineering is almost entirely the art of **not moving bytes** — reusing them in place (prefix caching), never materialising them (FlashAttention), shrinking them (quantization, latent caches), or proving they need not exist (eviction with recall handled elsewhere). Movement is the failure mode; stillness is the optimisation.

## Chapter 3 — KV Cache: The Working Memory of a Transformer

*This chapter synthesises `memory/kv-cache-design.md` — the corpus's largest memory dossier — with the paper dossiers it rests on. It is the book's longest chapter because the KV cache is where the wall, the hierarchy, and the allocator meet in one object.*

### 3.1 What the cache is, from first principles

A decoder-only Transformer generating token *t+1* lets the new token's query attend to the keys and values of all previous tokens. Those keys and values are deterministic functions of the earlier tokens and the weights; recomputing them each step would make generation cubic in sequence length. The KV cache is the memoisation of that observation: prefill computes each token's keys and values once and stores them; decode computes one new token's pair, appends it, and reads the stored history. Per-step cost drops from re-running the prefix to O(1) new work plus an O(*t*) *read* — the cache converts an impossible compute bill into a merely large memory bill (KV dossier §1.1). Three consequences shape everything:

1. **The cache is exact, not a summary.** Attention over a full cache is mathematically the attention the model would compute from scratch. Every lossy technique below (eviction, quantization) is a deliberate departure from exactness and must be graded as one.
2. **The cache is append-mostly and read-whole.** Entries are written once, in order, never modified, and read in full at every subsequent step. Almost every layout decision exploits this; almost every complication (branching, editing, sharing) is a place the pattern breaks.
3. **The cache is per-sequence state, not model state.** Weights are shared by every user of the model; the cache belongs to one conversation. That is why sessions, privacy, persistence, and preemption live here and not in the weights.

### 3.2 The size formula and its worked economies

For a standard attention layer, one token contributes one key and one value vector per KV head:

```
bytes per token = 2 × L × H_kv × D × b
  (K+V) × layers × KV heads × head dimension × bytes per element
```

Worked examples (corpus arithmetic from published configurations, f16/bf16, *b* = 2; KV dossier §1.2):

| Model | L | H_kv | D | Bytes/token | 4K tokens | 32K tokens | 128K tokens |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Llama-2-7B (MHA) | 32 | 32 | 128 | 524,288 B = 512 KiB | 2 GiB | 16 GiB | 64 GiB |
| Llama-3.1-8B (GQA) | 32 | 8 | 128 | 131,072 B = 128 KiB | 0.5 GiB | 4 GiB | 16 GiB |
| Llama-3.1-70B (GQA) | 80 | 8 | 128 | 327,680 B = 320 KiB | 1.25 GiB | 10 GiB | 40 GiB |

Cross-checks inside the corpus agree: the 8B row reproduces the llama.cpp dossier's independently derived 128 KiB/token; the 7B row reproduces the Prompt Cache paper's reported ~0.50 MB/token for Llama-7B; the MimirAlloc harness convention for TinyLlama-1.1B in f32 (2 × 22 × 4 × 64 × 4 = 45,056 B/token) is the same formula at smaller scale. The formula is not in dispute anywhere in the literature read; only its inputs vary.

Read by columns, the field's history falls out. **MHA → GQA is a 4× cache cut at 7–8B scale bought purely architecturally** — a runner cannot retrofit it, but must budget from the true `H_kv`, never the query-head count (budgeting a GQA model as MHA over-reserves 4×; the reverse OOMs). **Scale does not rescue you:** the 70B's per-token cost exceeds the 8B's. **Batch multiplies silently:** the H2O paper's motivating example — ~180 GB of KV for a 30B model at batch 128 × 1,024 tokens (`papers/h2o.md`) — is this arithmetic with the batch factor restored; at datacentre batch the cache dwarfs the weights by an order of magnitude.

**Why the cache, not the weights, binds at long context.** Weights are fixed; cache grows linearly without intrinsic limit. The crossover context is T* = weight bytes ÷ bytes-per-token. Llama-3.1-8B with Q4_K_M weights ≈ 4.9 GB and f16 cache at 131,072 B/token: T* ≈ 40,000 tokens — past ~40K, the cache is the larger resident object, for a model quantised precisely to fit small cards. The same 8B at f16 weights (~16 GiB): T* ≈ 131,072 tokens — serving the *advertised* 128K window at full precision requires roughly doubling the machine, and the second half is all cache. Capacity is only half the binding: every decode step *reads* the whole resident cache, so at T = 32K the 8B's cache contributes 4 GiB of the ~8.9 GiB read per generated token — nearly half the traffic is history, not model. Long context taxes twice: once in capacity, once in speed. There is no third tax and no other kind of win; every technique in §3.4–§3.6 attacks one or both.

### 3.3 Layout: units, addressing, orientation, sharing

Layout answers four separable questions (KV dossier §2):

**(i) Allocation geometry.** *Contiguous per-sequence arenas* (early llama.cpp, HF Transformers, Ollama's inherited model): trivial addressing, the fastest kernels — the PagedAttention paper measured its paged kernel at 20–26% higher attention latency than FasterTransformer's contiguous kernel — and ruinous accounting: memory pre-paid for context never used, no sharing, holes stranded on free; the paper's profiling found prior systems actually using only **20.4%–38.2%** of the KV memory they had allocated. *Blocked/paged tables* (PagedAttention, `papers/pagedattention-vllm.md`): one global pool of fixed-size blocks (16 tokens by default in vLLM), per-sequence block tables, on-demand allocation, reference counts — internal fragmentation bounded by one partial block per sequence, external fragmentation zero by construction, at the price of kernel indirection and all-or-nothing residency. *Radix trees* (SGLang's RadixAttention, `papers/radixattention-sglang.md`) are not a different geometry but a different *index* over the same paged pool: a compressed trie over token sequences making shared structure explicit, buying longest-prefix matching, leaf-LRU eviction that structurally protects shared trunks, and cache-aware scheduling. vLLM's production answer is deliberately flatter — a hash table over hash-chained full blocks. **Paging decides where bytes live; radix/hashing decides how reuse is found. They compose.** Every serious current system is a hybrid plus tiering: SGLang's HiCache extends its tree across GPU → host DRAM → external storage with *different layouts per tier* (layer-first on GPU for kernels, page-first on host/storage for transfer efficiency); vLLM V1 pairs its pool with an external KV-connector tier; llama.cpp pairs its cell pool with a RAM cache of idle slots and on-disk slot files. The principle to copy: **one addressing scheme (blocks), several residency tiers, layout chosen per tier.**

**(ii) Block size** — the one true tuning knob — trades internal fragmentation (average B/2 wasted slots per sequence), sharing granularity (reuse is claimable only in whole blocks; vLLM caches only full blocks), kernel/transfer efficiency, and bookkeeping (all O(tokens ÷ B)). The PagedAttention sweep found 16–128 tokens good on long ShareGPT traces, 16–32 on shorter Alpaca traces; SGLang defaults to a page of 1 token; tier transfers prefer 64–128. There is no universal optimum, and the corpus *disagrees with itself, honestly*: the IDEA-LEDGER (Open Questions, item 11) records PagedAttention's 16 (an A100 multi-request measurement), the vLLM dossier's argument for 32–64 at single-sequence scale, and the KV dossier's default of 16 with a sharing-shape caveat — to be settled by a sweep on the target, not by citation. Both defaults (16, 1) are measurements on someone else's hardware and traces; treating either as law is the error.

**(iii) K/V arrangement inside the block** is a kernel contract. Separate K and V tensors dominate production (clean read streams; K and V can differ in precision — they should; K can be stored transposed without disturbing V). The design rule for orientation: **choose the stored orientation for the decode read, and pay any transpose once at append time, never per read.** Across layers, layer-first suits kernels and token/page-first suits transfers; HiCache refuses to choose once and converts at tier boundaries. A second axis matters as much: in llama.cpp's cell design, **positions are logical, not physical** — a token is (sequence id, position), and range removal or shifting is metadata work, not memmove. A layout where position *is* the address makes every such operation a data movement.

**(iv) Who else can point at the same storage** — §3.4.

**The attention pattern is part of the layout.** Sliding-window layers need only the last W tokens: a ring buffer, not a growing array — different allocation, different eviction (the window *is* the eviction). SGLang's Unified Radix Cache gives sliding-window attention its own slot type, with tombstoned out-of-window slots. MLA-style latent caches dissolve K/V separation entirely (§3.6). A cache manager designed around "K blocks and V blocks" does not survive contact with MLA; one designed around "per-layer per-token byte cost, read from metadata" does.

**Where the runners sit** (KV dossier §2.5, synthesised from the runner dossiers): the field has converged on **paged pools + content-addressed reuse** at the serving end (vLLM, SGLang), while the local end (Ollama/llama.cpp) still runs **reserved windows + last-prompt reuse** — adequate at concurrency 1, and the gap between the two columns is precisely the list of things a local fork can adopt in miniature.

### 3.4 Sharing and reuse semantics

Sharing is where the cache stops being a private scratchpad and becomes a data structure with semantics — and failure modes. The governing invariant: two sequences may share cached state **only for a token-identical prefix, at identical positions, under identical model configuration** (weights, adapter, cache precision, RoPE scaling). Three production mechanisms find and hold the shared part (KV dossier §3.1): **last-prompt comparison** (llama.cpp/Ollama — one entry, no index, sufficient for append-only single-user chat); **hash-chained blocks** (vLLM — each full block named by a hash of parent hash + token IDs + extras including LoRA id and `cache_salt`; since v0.11 the default hash is SHA-256 at ~75 ns/token as documented; the chain means a block's name *is* its entire prefix); **radix tree** (SGLang — partial overlaps found by walking, nodes split on divergence, cache-aware ordering reaching ~96% of the optimal hit rate in the paper's evaluation, at <0.3% measured overhead on a no-reuse workload).

**Copy-on-write at branch points** is the operating-system answer inherited from PagedAttention: a block with refcount > 1 is never written in place; the writer gets a fresh block, copying only the partial boundary block. In serving, the canonical branches are parallel sampling and beam search (the paper's sharing savings reach ~55–66% of cache memory on beam-search workloads). In chat, they are product features: **swipe/regenerate** (everything up to the rejected reply is shared; the new reply is a sibling), **edit-and-continue** (branch at a mid-history point; retain prefix blocks, drop the rest), and **what-if forks** (SGLang's frontend makes `fork`/`join` a language primitive so the runtime can pin the parent path *before* the branch runs — the general lesson: a session API that announces an impending branch lets the cache manager protect the branch point). The correctness edge, stated plainly in the dossier and carried here: **CoW protects storage, not meaning.** Branches sharing bytes compute identically over the shared span only if sampling state, adapters, and configuration also match.

**Cross-session sharing** is where privacy and correctness bite. A prefix hit is measurably faster than a miss — a timing side channel by which a tenant can probe whether a guessed prefix is cached. vLLM's answer is the `cache_salt`, folded into the first block's hash so reuse happens only among requests carrying the same salt; its documentation warns that fast non-cryptographic hashes theoretically risk collisions with privacy consequences in multi-tenant settings — why SHA-256 is the default. llama.cpp's slot files make the same point from persistence: saved slot state *is* conversation state, with no per-user isolation boundary (a controlled experiment demonstrating cross-client slot-state recovery is reported in the llama.cpp dossier as a preprint finding, not a CVE, and is carried here with that grading). **The single-user inversion [synthesis judgement, from KV dossier §3.3]:** on a one-user machine the threat model inverts — cross-*session* sharing (same persona across chats, shared lore across characters) is a feature worth real memory on a 6 GB card. The correct local design is not "no sharing" but **sharing scoped by an explicit session/persona identity, never by accident**: reuse keys carry model + adapter + persona/session scope deliberately, playing the role `cache_salt` plays for tenants.

**Position-dependence: why KV is not freely movable.** A token's KV encodes *where* the token was, not just what it was. Under RoPE, each key is rotated by an angle determined by absolute position before caching; a key cached at position 1,000 is a different vector from the same token's key at position 200, and scores against the wrong rotation are simply wrong — not degraded, wrong. Consequences: prefix reuse works *because* positions are preserved; mid-prompt reuse is invalid by default; llama.cpp's context shift works because it *rewrites* positions (re-basing, not moving); and KVQuant's pre-RoPE key quantisation exists because RoPE mixes channels (§3.5). Prompt Cache (`papers/prompt-cache.md`) explores how far the binding can be bent — disjoint position-ID ranges per declared module, relying on models' *empirical* tolerance for discontinuous position IDs, measured to hold on its LongBench evaluation, model-dependent, requiring per-model validation. CacheBlend (`papers/cacheblend.md`) is the exception that proves the rule: it reuses chunks at new positions and its entire contribution is the repair bill — recompute the highest-deviation tokens (~15% in its experiments) layer by layer, pipelined under I/O. Read correctly, CacheBlend does not show position doesn't matter; it *prices* how much it matters. **Design rule (KV dossier §3.4):** any feature reusing KV at a different position than it was computed at is proposing an approximation, and must ship with a named repair or tolerance argument and a quality measurement against full prefill on the target model. Features reusing at identical positions are exact and need no such burden.

### 3.5 Precision: the cache is a numerical object

Cache precision is the only factor in the size formula a runner can change for a given model — the highest-leverage knob in this chapter, with the sharpest quality cliff. Halving bytes-per-element halves *both* taxes (capacity and per-step read bandwidth).

The ladder (KV dossier §5.1): f32 (2× f16 bytes; reference/harness use) → f16/bf16 (1×; the default almost everywhere) → fp8/q8_0-class int8 (~0.5×; llama.cpp q8_0 ≈ 34 B per 32 elements, taking a 32K-context 8B cache from 4 GiB to ~2.1 GiB; Ollama characterises q8_0's cost as "very small, usually unnoticeable") → q4_0-class int4 (~0.25–0.28×; Ollama: "small-to-medium" cost, "more noticeable at large contexts"; vLLM documents no INT4 KV). Two precisions at the same byte width are not the same choice: f16 spends bits on mantissa over a narrow exponent range; bf16 on range with a coarse mantissa. Both cost identical bytes; the choice is pure error-shaping and should follow the model's own training precision unless measurements say otherwise. A coupling must be surfaced to users: in the llama.cpp/Ollama stack, quantised cache types are tied to Flash Attention being active (per-backend coupling varies by build) — a fork must report the *effective* cache type in force, not merely the requested one.

**Scaling granularity: per-channel keys, per-token values.** Uniform quantisation fails structurally (KIVI/KVQuant line, `papers/kivi.md`, `papers/kvquant.md`): in keys, a few *channels* carry consistently large magnitudes across tokens; in values, outliers spread per token. A single per-tensor scale is destroyed by key channels it must accommodate. The established remedies, on which the two independent lines agree: keys per-channel; values per-token; KVQuant's pre-RoPE key quantisation (rotation would smear the per-channel structure); outlier sparing (KVQuant keeps ~1% of elements in full precision; reported <0.1 perplexity degradation at 3-bit with sparing on WikiText-2/C4 across LLaMA/Llama-2/Llama-3/Mistral, A100-class serving context); KIVI's complementary full-precision residual window for recent tokens. The retrieval evidence separates the schemes where perplexity does not: on passkey retrieval to 32K (LLaMA-2-7B-32K), KVQuant's 2-bit-with-sparing scores 0.98–1.00 across lengths where KIVI-2 scores 0.68–0.76; on RULER, FP16 56.40 → KVQuant-3bit-1% 53.65 versus KIVI-2 39.78 (`papers/kvquant.md` §5). KIVI's own headline is equally real in its regime: 2.6× less peak memory than FP16 including weights, up to 4× larger batch, 2.35–3.47× throughput on Llama-2-7B (single A100-80GB, ShareGPT-derived workload), with LongBench averages essentially held at 2 bits (44.52 → 44.27) — and its honest exception: Falcon-7B, already head-compressed by MQA, needed 4-bit to hold accuracy. **Transferable principle: K and V are different distributions and must be quantised — and budgeted, and if necessary precision-tiered — independently.**

**Why quantisation degrades more at long context** (KV dossier §5.3), three compounding structural mechanisms: (1) every step reads the whole history, so an early token's key error is paid at *every* later position and perturbs the shared softmax denominator — errors interact rather than average out; (2) softmax amplifies key errors competitively — at long context the competition is among thousands of near-ties, precisely where small score noise reorders winners (the same near-tie fragility Aesir's oracle grading watches for, Chapter 9); (3) the important tokens *are* the outliers — sinks and heavy hitters are numerically the extreme entries quantisation represents worst. Hence mixed-precision policies: sinks/persona head and recent window in f16, bulk at int8, cold middle (if anywhere) at int4 — precision following measured importance. **[The tier boundaries are a design proposal — speculation as a prescription in the source dossier, though each ingredient is individually evidenced.]** Grading discipline: cache-precision claims must be graded at the longest contexts the feature will actually run. A q4_0 cache fine at 4K and confabulating at 32K is a context-dependent defect, and the UI should say which contexts each tier is validated for.

### 3.6 Architectural answers versus runtime policies — the chapter's thesis

Everything above divides into two families, and confusing them is the most common design error in this space:

**Architectural answers** change the formula itself and are fixed at training time. The MHA → MQA → GQA → MLA lineage is a sequence of attacks on `H_kv` (and, for MLA, on the whole `H_kv × D` product):

- **MQA** (Shazeer 2019): one KV head — the endpoint of the sharing spectrum.
- **GQA** (Ainslie et al., EMNLP 2023; `papers/gqa.md`): grouped sharing. On T5.1.1 XXL (TPUv4, the paper's testbed): MHA 1.51 s/sample at average score 47.2; GQA-8 0.28 s/sample at 47.1 — ~5.4× faster within 0.1 points; MQA marginally faster (0.24 s) at 46.6. Uptraining an existing checkpoint for 5% of original steps buys most of the conversion. The paper contains no decoder-only experiments — the now-universal decoder-only GQA deployments are the field's extrapolation, and the dossier says so.
- **MLA** (DeepSeek-V2, 2024; `papers/deepseek-v2-mla.md`): cache one compressed latent per token per layer instead of per-head vectors, plus a small decoupled RoPE key. The controlled ablation is the cleanest evidence in the paper: same-architecture MoE pairs differing only in attention — small MoE KV per token 110.6K elements (MHA) → 15.6K (MLA), 14%, with MMLU *rising* 48.7 → 50.0; large MoE 860.2K → 34.6K, 4%, MMLU 57.5 → 59.0. Compression as a quality *gain* — plausibly a regularisation effect of the low-rank bottleneck — is what makes MLA more than an engineering trick. The full-model headline (KV reduced 93.3% versus DeepSeek 67B; generation throughput 5.76×) is a *system* result (MLA + MoE sparsity + FP8 + KV quantisation + serving stack on 8× H800) and must never be quoted as MLA alone.
- **Sliding-window and sparse attention** bound the token factor per layer; **SSM/recurrent layers** replace the growing term with fixed state (§3.7).

**Runtime policies** change how a given formula's bytes are laid out, shared, compressed, evicted, and tiered — §§3.3–3.5 and §3.8. They are available to any runner, for any model, today.

**Thesis.** *Architecture decides what a token of context costs; runtime decides how honestly and how fully you pay it.* A runner cannot retrofit GQA or MLA — but a runner that budgets from query heads instead of KV heads, reserves contiguous windows in a paged world, or truncates silently has failed on its own side of the line regardless of the model's architecture. Conversely, no runtime policy recovers what an MHA architecture spends: at 512 KiB/token, policy is triage. Roadmaps that promise runtime fixes for architectural costs — or blame runtimes for them — misassign the bill. The split also predicts where progress comes from: architectural wins arrive with model generations (and strand old checkpoints), runtime wins arrive with engine releases (and apply to every checkpoint already on disk). A local runner's leverage is overwhelmingly on the runtime side; its *accounting* must be fluent in the architectural side.

### 3.7 The hybrid complication: the cache is no longer the whole memory

A growing class of models interleaves attention layers (growing, token-addressable, exact KV) with recurrent/SSM layers (fixed-size state, updated in place, *not* token-addressable — no operation removes token 300's contribution; the only lifecycle operation is checkpoint-and-restore-whole). Serving memory becomes `KV bytes (attention layers only) + state bytes (fixed, per sequence)`, with opposite scaling and completely different reuse mechanics (KV dossier §6).

The corpus's first-hand evidence is Aesir's Qwen3.5 work (`aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md` §3.2, from campaign records of 2026-11-14): qwen35 27B — 64 blocks, hidden 5,120, 24 query heads / 4 KV heads, head dim 256, context 262,144, most layers recurrent Gated-DeltaNet, full attention at intervals. One attention layer costs 2 × 4 × 256 × 2 = 4,096 B/token; at an illustrative every-4th-block interval (16 of 64 layers — the interval is *not* fixed in the record read, and the dossier flags the total as illustrative while the per-layer figure and method are exact), the hybrid rate is 64 KiB/token — half the dense 8B's rate from a model with 3× the parameters. One full SSM+conv snapshot ≈ **158.9 MB**, fixed, per checkpoint; Aesir's store bound was 4 snapshots per session (~636 MB before a single KV byte). The reuse mechanism that works (Q35-5, recorded as proven): snapshot hybrid state at a prefix boundary; restore is **bit-exact** (SSM/conv/KV diffs 0.0); a stateless resend reused 72 of 90 tokens via snapshots after residency was destroyed.

SGLang arrived at the same place from the serving side (`runners/sglang.md` §5.4): separate pools (per-token KV; per-request fixed state, sized statically to eliminate runtime OOM from state allocation), checkpointed prefix reuse (MambaRadixCache — snapshots attached to radix nodes, copied on write, matched at the deepest node holding *both* KV prefix and state checkpoint, with *two* LRU lists because a checkpoint can be evicted from any node while KV must go leaf-to-root), generalised as the Unified Radix Cache — one tree, one slot per component (FULL / SWA / MAMBA), reuse decided by voting at the deepest boundary every component accepts. llama.cpp's `llama_memory_i` abstraction makes the same recognition quietly. Three independent codebases converging on "memory is a sum of per-kind components behind one manager" is as close to a settled design fact as this young area has.

Design consequences (KV dossier §6.4): budget per component or the fit-checker lies by hundreds of MB per hybrid session; eviction splits (no H2O for a fused state — importance scoring cannot reach inside it; whether a state can be approximately summarised for eviction is an open research question, flagged as speculation if asserted); branching costs a state copy (a swipe is nearly free on dense models, ~159 MB at 27B hybrid scale); non-prefix reuse mostly dies (CacheBlend-style fusion presumes token-addressable state); persistence must save the (KV + state) pair atomically at a boundary, versioned together.

### 3.8 Lifecycle: growth, eviction, preemption, persistence

**Growth.** Three policies cover the field: reserve-the-maximum (Ollama/llama.cpp slots — admission decided once, waste held hostage); grow-on-demand from a pool (vLLM, SGLang — admission re-decided every step, which is what makes continuous batching and preemption both necessary and possible; note the pool itself is pre-sized at startup in both systems — "on demand" describes block assignment, not OS allocation); checkpoint-assisted growth (restore points recorded as the sequence grows). The two-level shape — one big up-front arena, fine-grained assignment inside it — is the single most reusable systems idea in this chapter.

**Eviction** operates at two levels that must not be confused: *cache-level* (dropping retained-for-reuse prefixes; victim recomputable later; settled answer: LRU over unreferenced leaves / free-queue order) and *sequence-level* (removing tokens from a live sequence's attention reach; the model permanently loses them). The sequence-level taxonomy, with failure modes (KV dossier §4.2):

- **Stop** at the window edge (llama-server default) — honest but useless for long chat.
- **Positional shift** (keep first `n_keep`, discard a middle span, re-base positions; Ollama layer 2, `num_keep` = 4 by default) — keeps a *count*, not the content: a persona living past token 4 is discarded silently.
- **Front truncation / FIFO** (Ollama layer 1, message-level) — StreamingLLM's collapse: evicting the first tokens destroys the attention sink and perplexity explodes (Llama-2-13B window attention 5158 vs 5.40 sink+window on PG-19; `papers/streamingllm.md`).
- **Sink + window** (StreamingLLM: pin the first few tokens — 4 sink tokens sufficed on Llama-2-7B ablations — FIFO inside a rolling window; stable generation over 4M+ tokens, up to 22.2× faster per token than sliding-window-with-recomputation, single A6000) — stability without recall; the evicted middle is gone forever and must never be sold as memory.
- **Attention-scored** (H2O: heavy hitters by accumulated attention + recent window; >95% attention sparsity measured; at a 20% budget, quality near full cache on its suites, throughput up to 29× over offloading-heavy baselines on a single A100-80GB — read as "a small in-GPU cache beats spilling," not as a kernel effect). Irreversible and backward-looking; needs scores fused kernels may not expose; a once-mentioned name that never drew attention is exactly the adversarial victim.
- **Observation-window scored** (SnapKV: score prompt tokens by attention from a trailing window at end of prefill, keep top-B per head; ~3.6× generation speedup and 8.2× memory efficiency at its A100 operating points, needle tests to 380K context from a 1,024-entry cache — with an honest weak spot: long-document summarisation, GovReport 27.97 → 19.79 at the tightest budget) and **PyramidKV** (allocate B unevenly across layers; at comfortable budgets allocation barely matters — LongBench 41.49 vs full-cache 41.46 at 2,048 average — while at starved budgets, average 64/layer, it earns its keep: 34.76 vs SnapKV 33.05, with TREC 58.00 vs 38.50 as the flagship gap).

Two cross-cutting judgements: the policies **compose** — the defensible long-chat stack is pinned head (persona + sinks) + heavy-hitter/recent hybrid middle + an *external* memory (summary/retrieval, Chapter 7) for facts, because no eviction policy provides recall; and **eviction granularity should be the block** — scoring may be per-token, but evicting inside blocks reintroduces the fragmentation paging exists to remove.

**Preemption.** When resident sequences collectively exceed the pool, a running sequence must yield. *Recompute* (vLLM V1's only mechanism — V0's swap was removed in the V1 redesign; all-or-nothing per sequence) wins at small block sizes where swapping means many small PCIe transfers; the two are comparable around block 16–64 (PagedAttention paper's costing). Prefix caching is recompute's hidden ally: retained blocks may make resumption partially a re-hit. *Swap to host RAM* pays bytes ÷ interconnect twice; on a single-user laptop the calculus inverts — host RAM is idle, recompute on a small GPU is slow in absolute terms — so llama.cpp's shape is the right local one: a bounded RAM cache (default 8,192 MiB) for *idle* slots; **spill the idle, never the running**. SGLang's *retraction* is recompute integrated with the radix tree: blocks return to the tree as reusable cache. The decision variable is `recompute cost ÷ transfer cost` at the sequence's length on the actual hardware — it flips with length and cache warmth.

**Persistence.** llama.cpp is the corpus's working example, and its dossier's separation of three routinely conflated mechanisms is itself a design lesson: CLI prompt-cache files (full inference state after prefill; same model/context only; no guarantee of identical subsequent tokens even at fixed seed; non-portable binary format), server slot save/restore (disabled by default; every failure returns the same generic 400), and in-memory prompt caching (not persistence at all). The measured economics dominate: Qwen3-1.7B, 7,289-token prompt, CPU-only Debian VM — **cold prefill 160.7 s vs restore 2.14 s, file 836 MB** (~114,700 B per saved token for that architecture/cache type); a second measurement: 5K-token chat 9.9 s cold vs 1.4 s restored (~7×). Restore is a file read, not a computation. Design judgements: a session file must carry its own **validity envelope** (model identity + quant, cache precision, tokeniser/template version, token count) and refuse loudly and distinctly on mismatch; file size *is* cache size (a 32K f16 8B session file is 4 GiB — persistence policy is a capacity decision); a saved cache is a **privacy object** — the conversation in resumable form — inheriting the conversation's storage protections; and the alternative framing (HiCache L3, LMCache) treats persistence as the cache's bottom tier — content-addressed, evictable, shared by prefix — which wins if cross-session prefix reuse from disk is ever wanted.

### 3.9 The worked case: what the arithmetic decides on a real small card

The KV dossier's §7 works the decision procedure end-to-end for purplelongship (RTX 2060 Max-Q, 6 GiB VRAM, single-user long chat, Llama-3.1-8B class, Q4_K_M weights ≈ 4.9 GiB). Budget: 6,144 MiB − 5,018 MiB weights − 512 MiB planning reserve (a planning figure, flagged in the source as awaiting a measured replacement) = **614 MiB for KV**. Precision ladder: f16 → 4,912 tokens (fails even an 8K minimum); q8_0 (69,632 B/token) → 9,246 tokens (ship `num_ctx` 8,192 with ~13% headroom); q4_0 (36,864 B/token) → 17,465 tokens (16K reachable, at the precision tier whose long-context cost is documented as noticeable — an explicitly labelled experiment only). Note what the arithmetic forbids: stock Ollama behaviour on this card (VRAM tier → `num_ctx` 4,096 at f16) is not a conservative version of this decision but a *different* one — spending the whole KV budget on precision the workload cannot perceive while halving the context it can, then truncating silently when even that fills. The alternative the procedure surfaces: a 3B-class GQA model (`kv_rate` 114,688 B/token f16, Q4 weights ≈ 2.0 GiB) leaves 3,584 MiB for KV → **32,768 tokens at full f16 precision**. On a 6 GB card, *context is bought with model size more cheaply than with cache precision* — a trade the UI should show as arithmetic, not folklore. No tok/s figure is asserted for this card anywhere in the dossier: its sustained bandwidth was not measured in any record available to that pass, and the dossier prescribes the STREAM-style measurement instead. That refusal is Chapter 9 in miniature.

The dossier's seven design rules distil to three strongest: **budget from `kv_rate`, never from parameter count or advertised context** (most small-card misery traces to budgets nobody computed); **eviction is a declared composition, never an accident of two layers** (the persona-beheading failure is a policy-composition bug, not a capacity fact); **one arena, blocks inside it, no per-block host allocation** (the discipline that makes sharing, eviction composition, and persistence implementable at all — and the point where our own allocator evidence, Chapter 5, most directly disciplines the design).

## Chapter 4 — Attention as Memory Traffic

### 4.1 FlashAttention read purely as memory engineering

Strip the FlashAttention lineage of its kernel fame and what remains is a sequence of discoveries about *where bytes should never go* (`papers/flashattention.md`, `papers/flashattention-2.md`, `papers/flashattention-3.md`, `papers/flashinfer.md`).

**FlashAttention (Dao et al., NeurIPS 2022)** begins from a diagnosis, not an optimisation: standard attention materialises the N×N score and probability matrices in HBM between steps — O(N²) memory, each step re-reading and re-writing large intermediates through the slowest level of the hierarchy — while most of its operations (softmax, masking, elementwise work) are memory-bound, their runtime set by bytes moved between HBM (~1.5–2.0 TB/s on A100) and chip, not by arithmetic (SRAM ~19 TB/s estimated, 192 KB per SM). The approximate-attention literature had attacked FLOP count, often to linear complexity, yet frequently failed to beat standard attention in wall-clock time. The authors' explanation: those methods optimised the wrong quantity. The fix computes *exact* attention — no approximation — while never letting S or P exist in HBM: **tiling** (blocks of K/V loaded into SRAM, blocks of Q streamed past them, partial results accumulated on chip; extra memory beyond inputs and output is O(N) — one running maximum and one running sum per row — instead of O(N²)) plus **online softmax** (softmax computed incrementally, rescaling as each block's true maximum emerges) plus recomputation of attention in the backward pass rather than storing it. "IO-awareness" is the paper's name for the method: account for reads and writes between levels of GPU memory. It is the kernel-level statement of Chapter 1's system-level claim.

**FlashAttention-2 (2023)** changes no mathematics — same exact attention, tiling, online softmax, IO complexity — and is therefore the lineage's purest lesson: once HBM traffic is solved, the bottleneck moves *inside* the chip, to scheduling, occupancy, and non-matmul work. Its three moves: fewer non-matmul FLOPs (on A100, FP16/BF16 matmul peaks at 312 TFLOP/s while non-matmul FP32 arithmetic peaks at 19.5 TFLOP/s — each non-matmul FLOP effectively costs 16× a matmul FLOP, so softmax bookkeeping can dominate while being a small fraction of the count; the output accumulator is kept unscaled through the inner loop to cut rescaling), better work partitioning across warps, and a loop order that raises occupancy. **[Synthesis judgement]** FA2 is the roofline's corollary in practice: removing one ceiling reveals the next, and each successive ceiling is at a smaller, faster level of the hierarchy — the wall recurses.

**FlashAttention-3 (2024)** redesigns the same exact computation around Hopper asynchrony and FP8: producer–consumer warp specialisation (producer warps issue only TMA loads into a circular multi-stage shared-memory buffer; consumer warpgroups compute only, with WGMMA; register budgets rebalanced to the roles), a pingpong schedule overlapping two consumer warpgroups, and low-precision units engaged deliberately. The framing in its dossier: FA2's schedule "ignores that modern GPUs are asynchronous, multi-unit machines, and ignores the low-precision units entirely." **FlashInfer** (`papers/flashinfer.md`) generalises the substrate into a kernel library — composable attention kernels (paged, ragged, cascade) that serving engines build on — the point where attention-as-memory-traffic becomes infrastructure rather than a paper.

The through-line for this book: attention was never a compute problem wearing a memory costume. It was a memory problem that FLOP-counting hid. Every generation of the lineage wins by moving fewer bytes, staging bytes closer, or overlapping the movement that remains — and none wins by approximating the mathematics (until, separately and explicitly, the KV-compression family of Chapter 3 chooses approximation as a *cache* policy, graded as one).

### 4.2 Prefix caching: memory reuse across time

If FlashAttention is reuse across *space* (bytes already on chip, never sent back), prefix caching is reuse across *time*: bytes computed for one request, kept, and recognised as the beginning of the next. The mechanisms were detailed in §3.4 (last-prompt comparison; hash-chained blocks; radix trees). This section prices them and places the three research systems that pushed reuse beyond the prefix.

**The value, measured.** In the independent consumer-hardware measurement cited by the SGLang dossier, a 94%-cached prompt cut TTFT ~13× on a laptop-class chip and ~5× on an RTX 3090; an MLX field case shows a cached turn at ~1.4 s versus ~29.5 s uncached (IDEA-LEDGER #5, evidence grade B for the consumer figures). Fleet hit rates of 50–99% are paper-reported on datacentre workloads and are *not* the local expectation; the local expectation is the shape — later-turn TTFT falls by roughly the cached fraction. Our own fleet's pain quantifies the disease from the other side: Ollama-via-Voxta re-evaluating a 14,712-token prompt from cold cost **52.1 s before the first word** on purpleraider (`memory/unified-memory.md` §3.4, from the day's records) — decode was never the disease; re-processing was. Aesir's residency work (HS-3) is the same cure at engine scale: turn 2 reusing tokens instead of re-evaluating, 610 ms versus 825 ms in its small proof, with the large-chat magnitude an inference, graded as such in the ledger.

**Prompt Cache (Gim et al., MLSys 2024; `papers/prompt-cache.md`)** pushes reuse from prefixes to *modules*: declared prompt components (system text, document chunks, few-shot blocks) precomputed once, stored, and spliced into new prompts at reserved, disjoint position-ID ranges — the position-binding bend of §3.4, resting on empirical tolerance for discontinuous position IDs. Reported: TTFT improvement 8× (GPU) to 60× (CPU) in the abstract's accounting; in detail, 1.5–3× with modules in CPU memory and 5–10× in GPU memory on LongBench (Llama-7B class, ~5K-token prompts; RTX 4090, A40, A100), up to 70× on an Intel i9-13900K CPU system and 20× on a Ryzen 9 7950X — the authors attribute the CPU gap largely to memory bandwidth, which is this book's thesis arriving uninvited in someone else's ablation. Accuracy on LongBench was comparable to baseline, with per-dataset outliers in *both* directions — the signature of an approximation that must be validated per model, exactly as §3.4's design rule demands.

**CacheBlend (Yao et al., EuroSys 2025; `papers/cacheblend.md`)** prices non-prefix reuse for RAG: chunks reused wherever they land, with the highest-deviation tokens' KV recomputed (~15% ratio in its experiments) layer by layer to re-inject cross-attention and position context, pipelined against loading so the repair hides under I/O. Reported on Mistral-7B / Yi-34B / Llama-70B (2× A40, 128 GB RAM, NVMe at 4.8 GB/s): TTFT reduced 2.2–3.3× and throughput up 2.8–5× versus full recompute, quality within 0.01–0.03 of full recompute and *above* naive full reuse by 0.1–0.2 F1 on QA tasks. The recompute ratio is a tuned, workload-dependent parameter, not a constant — and, per §3.7, the technique presumes token-addressable state and therefore mostly does not transfer to hybrid recurrent models.

**The discipline that makes any of it hit.** Prefix reuse is brittle to rendering: if the application rebuilds prompts with history in a different order, or injects a changing timestamp or random seed early in the prompt, the match breaks at that token and everything after it misses (RadixAttention dossier's warning). Hence IDEA-LEDGER #13 — **byte-stable prompt rendering as a caching requirement** (stable content first, volatile content last) — and #14 — **stable-order, whole-block memory injection under a token budget** (Chapter 7's read path obeys the same law for the same reason). **[Synthesis judgement]** Reuse systems fail socially before they fail technically: the cache is exact, the index is correct, and the client quietly changes one early byte per turn. Prompt assembly discipline is a prerequisite, not an optimisation.

## Chapter 5 — Allocation, the Discipline

*This chapter synthesises `memory/allocator-lineages.md` and places `memory/mimiralloc-our-own-proven-work.md` inside it. The allocator dossier's warning governs the whole chapter: a runner is not a general allocator workload. Weights are a few huge, long-lived extents; the KV cache is a growing pool of identically shaped blocks; per-step scratch is arena-shaped; only the surrounding server code looks like a general `malloc` workload. Each shape has a different right allocator, and using the general one for all of them is how runners waste VRAM and RSS.*

### 5.1 The lineage: what each generation solved

An allocator's job, stated three ways that pull against each other: **space** (waste as little as possible, now and after thousands of interleaved frees), **time** (a handful of instructions in the common case), **concurrency** (many threads without serialising on one lock or cache line). Each generation answers the previous generation's failure on one axis (allocator dossier §1):

- **Single-heap, exact-fit era — dlmalloc** (Doug Lea, from 1987). Boundary tags give O(1) neighbour coalescing; 128 size-sorted bins approximate best-fit; a top/wilderness chunk extends at the break; very large requests bypass via `mmap`. Failure modes that define the family: lock serialisation under threads; best-fit slivers; a 16/24-byte minimum-chunk tax on tiny objects; **top-pinning** — one long-lived allocation near the top prevents all trimming beneath it, so RSS reflects the high-water mark, not live data; and no compaction, ever — pointers are raw addresses, so fragmentation once created can only be worked around, never undone.
- **Thread era — Hoard (2000), tcmalloc (2005–).** Hoard proved per-processor sharding scales where a global lock does not, that false sharing is an *allocator* bug, and that any sharding scheme without a rebalancing rule blows up: superblocks (8 KiB, one size class, one owning heap) migrate to a global heap when a heap's emptiness crosses its threshold (f = 1/4 in the paper), bounding retained memory within a factor of the single-heap optimum. tcmalloc industrialised the shape in three tiers — per-thread, now per-CPU, caches (lock-free fast path; per-CPU mode via Linux restartable sequences); a transfer cache and central free lists moving objects in batches; a page heap of *spans* (runs of pages, each either one large object or many equal small objects of one recorded class) with a radix pagemap answering `free(ptr)`'s "where does this belong." Size classes number roughly 60–90 depending on page-size variant and version — the dossier flags version drift explicitly: folklore quoting "88 classes, 32 KiB" without a version is quoting the oldest document as if current.
- **Scale-and-return era — jemalloc (2006), modern tcmalloc.** Thread caches alone left servers growing RSS forever and arena counts themselves becoming a fragmentation source at hundreds of threads. jemalloc's signature contribution is treating **returning memory to the OS as a first-class, tunable, time-based policy**: freed pages move dirty → muzzy (lazily purged, `MADV_FREE`-style) → retained (virtual kept, physical released), on decay timers (`dirty_decay_ms` / `muzzy_decay_ms`), optionally on background threads, forcible per arena via `mallctl`. Its arenas partition the heap independently (and, used explicitly, let an application segregate lifetimes — one arena for weights, one for KV, one for scratch); its **extent hooks** let an application replace how an arena obtains, commits, purges, splits, and merges backing memory — the cleanest documented mechanism in any general allocator for telling the allocator about special memory (pinned, huge-page, GPU-adjacent) instead of bypassing it. Its introspection (`mallctl` statistics decomposing allocated / active / mapped / retained / resident) is the measurement surface Chapter 9 builds on.
- **Locality-first era — mimalloc (2019).** Even with arenas and thread caches, one free list per size class scatters objects allocated together across the heap and forces atomic coordination on cross-thread free — the dominant pattern in reference-counted languages, where producer and consumer are often different threads. mimalloc shards the free list itself: many small lists, one set *per page*, and **three lists per page** (allocation list; local-free list, plain pointer writes; thread-free list, a single CAS on a separate head) so local alloc, local free, and remote free never contend on the same head. Maintenance cannot be starved: because the allocation list is finite, every bounded number of allocations exhausts it and takes the slow path that collects the other lists. A page that becomes empty is scheduled for **eager purge** (decommit drops RSS immediately; a purge delay — documented defaults differ by major version, 10 ms in v1/v2 option tables, 1,000 ms in v3's README; name the version — trades thrash against footprint). The tech report's Redis result (7% faster than tcmalloc, 14% than jemalloc on that benchmark) is quoted in the dossier as the report's own claim on its own suite, not a universal ranking.

### 5.2 The fixed-shape world: slab, arena, pool, bump

Running parallel to the general lineage since Bonwick's slab allocator (USENIX 1994) is the refusal of generality (allocator dossier §3). When size is fixed and lifetime is uniform, allocation degenerates: allocate = pop a free list or bump a pointer; free = push back, or do nothing per object and reset the whole region at the lifetime boundary.

- **Slab.** A cache per object type; slabs (pages carved into equal objects of one size, tracked full/partial/free); and Bonwick's second, equally important idea — **object caching**: because the allocator knows the type, it preserves an object's *constructed* state between uses, paying construction cost once per slot, not once per use. Reported in the lineage: internal fragmentation bounded (~1/8 in the SunOS implementation) and system fragmentation falling from 46% to 14% in Solaris 2.4 versus the allocator it replaced (the design lineage's own reported figures). Magazines (2001) added per-CPU batching; Linux SLUB (default since 2.6.23, sole slab allocator since 6.8) absorbed the per-CPU lesson — one current slab per CPU per cache, lockless pop, the free-list next-pointer stored inside the free object itself, costing no extra memory.
- **Arenas/regions/bump.** A chain of large backing blocks and one bump pointer: allocation is a bounds check plus an add; free is zero per object, O(1) per arena reset; external fragmentation within the arena is impossible in the Robson sense (nothing is ever freed out of order — nothing is freed at all). The failure mode is symmetric: **lifetime leakage** — one object that must outlive the arena pins the whole arena; mixing lifetimes silently converts peak-lifetime memory into whole-phase memory. Production homes: compiler zones, per-request scratch, per-frame engine data. Go's user-arena experiment is cited in the dossier as an experiment, never as a Go feature.
- **Pools** are the middle point: slab-like caches of fixed-size reusable objects with individual checkout/checkin.

**KV block pools ARE slab allocators.** The allocator dossier states this as identity, not analogy, field by field: cache ↔ pool; one object type/size ↔ one block shape (B tokens × layers × KV heads × head dim × 2 × dtype bytes); slab pages carved into equal objects ↔ a GPU region carved at startup into equal blocks (vLLM default B = 16); free list ↔ free list of block IDs; per-type accounting ↔ per-pool accounting (the block table). The differences are equally precise: indirection is mandatory (a block table consulted by the attention kernel, because blocks are deliberately non-contiguous — PagedAttention is slab allocation *plus paging*); and the waste killed is mostly *reservation* waste (the paper's ~20–40% utilisation of contiguous baselines versus ~under 4% waste paged, §3.3). Chapter 3's Rule 2 — one arena, blocks inside it, no per-block host allocation — is this section's theorem applied.

### 5.3 Fragmentation theory: Robson, Wilson, and the hard-ceiling case

Definitions that must not be blurred (allocator dossier §4.1): **internal** fragmentation is bytes allocated but unused inside a block (rounding, alignment, headers, a KV block's unfilled tail) — measurable exactly. **External** fragmentation is free bytes that exist in total but in no single piece large enough for the next request — meaningful only relative to a request distribution. **Retention** is neither: free bytes the allocator *could* return but holds for reuse (dirty pages awaiting decay, thread caches). Retention inflates RSS; fragmentation inflates failure at a ceiling. Different diseases, different cures — and conflating them is the most common measurement error in the field (Chapter 9).

**Robson's bound.** John Robson proved (1971; refined 1974/1977) that for online allocation without relocation, an adversary can force *any* deterministic allocator to need memory proportional to M · log₂(n), where M is maximum live data and n is the ratio of largest to smallest block size; in modern language the optimal deterministic competitive ratio is Θ(log M). Randomisation improves the lower bound only modestly. The adversarial construction is a comb: allocate many blocks, free every second one, and free space is diced into holes each too small for the next requested size while total free space is ample. Three practical readings: the bound is about *range of sizes*, so segregating huge allocations from small ones (jemalloc's oversize arena, tcmalloc's large path, dlmalloc's `mmap` path) directly lowers the worst-case multiplier — segregation is theory applied; **fixed-size allocation escapes the bound entirely** (n = 1, the log term vanishes — the theoretical statement of why slab/KV pools work); and **relocation escapes it** (a compacting collector can coalesce all free space — one deep reason GC languages tolerate patterns that would wreck a manual heap, and why non-moving collectors escape only partially).

**Wilson et al. (IWMM 1995)** is the field's empirical correction to its own theory: real programs are not adversaries. Size distributions are heavily skewed, lifetimes often LIFO-correlated or phase-structured, allocation comes in ramps, peaks, and plateaus — and **fragmentation at peaks is what matters**, because a heap that fragments at its high-water mark sets the machine's memory requirement. Under real traces, good policies (best-fit variants, segregated storage with well-chosen classes) track close to optimal, far from Robson's worst case; the survey's enduring verdict is that **policy matters more than mechanism**. The dossier flags one folklore item in context: Knuth's "50% rule" is a first-fit steady-state analysis result, routinely quoted as a universal fragmentation constant and sourced nowhere as one. **[Synthesis judgement, after Wilson]** Measure the actual request stream — weights (few, huge, once), KV (one size, append-mostly), scratch (phase-lived), server glue (general) — and give each stream the policy its regularity rewards, rather than benchmarking one allocator on synthetic random traces and declaring a winner.

**Under a hard ceiling**, fragmentation costs correctness of admission, not just RSS. PyTorch's CUDA caching allocator is the publicly documented reference (allocator dossier §4.4): large segments from the driver carved into blocks internally; freed blocks cached, split, coalesced *within* a segment — but blocks in different segments can never merge, because each `cudaMalloc` is an independent driver allocation; cross-segment fragmentation is structural, exactly analogous to dlmalloc's unreturnable interior holes. Its OOM message institutionalises the discipline: allocated vs reserved-but-unallocated are named as distinct causes, and `expandable_segments` (one large virtual reservation per pool via CUDA virtual-memory APIs, physical pages mapped on demand at documented 2 MiB / 20 MiB granularities) removes the cross-segment barrier because all blocks now live in one virtual range where neighbours can always merge. The translation to runners is direct: weights first, contiguously, never interleaved with churn; KV slabbed so it cannot fragment internally at all; transient buffers arena'd per phase; what remains for a general extent policy is small — and that is the point. **Every subsystem moved to slab/arena is a subsystem Robson's adversary can no longer attack.**

### 5.4 Managed versus manual versus ownership — and the Go routing rule

Three answers to "who frees, and when" (allocator dossier §5.1): manual (programmer per block; no movement; use-after-free and leaks are program bugs); tracing GC (runtime by reachability; Go non-moving, modern Java mostly moving; collection cost and non-deterministic reclamation are the price); ownership/RAII/reference counting (compiler-inserted drops, deterministic; refcount traffic is the price, cycles need a backup). Rust standardises the *interface* — `GlobalAlloc` over an explicit `Layout` of size + alignment — not the allocator, and the ecosystem swaps mimalloc/jemalloc/tcmalloc/snmalloc/bump arenas freely. **[Judgement carried from the dossier]** Mojo/Aesir sits closest to the ownership column: deterministic lifetimes are available at compile time, so Aesir should spend them (arena/slab placement decided statically) rather than paying a general allocator to rediscover lifetimes at runtime.

**Go's runtime allocator** is documented as originally based on tcmalloc, since diverged (`src/runtime/malloc.go` design comment): ~70 size classes for small objects (≤32 KiB), a tiny allocator packing pointer-free sub-16-byte objects, `mspan` (8 KiB page runs of one class, free *bitmap*, scan/noscan distinction), per-P `mcache` (lock-free), per-class `mcentral`, and a global `mheap` over 64 MiB arenas; large objects allocate directly at the heap. Sweeping is the free path; a background scavenger returns idle pages' physical backing (`MADV_DONTNEED` by default on Linux since Go 1.16; virtual space is never unmapped), with `HeapInuse / HeapIdle / HeapReleased / HeapSys` exposing the distinctions precisely. Two properties drive the routing rule: the Go heap is **non-moving** (fully-empty spans coalesce; partially-live ones pin — span-pinning inherited from tcmalloc), and **every heap byte is GC-accounted** (pacing via `GOGC`/`GOMEMLIMIT` tuned for many small-to-medium objects, not multi-gigabyte arrays whose liveness a collector must re-verify every cycle).

**Why a Go runner's tensor/KV memory should live outside the GC heap [engineering judgement in the source dossier, reasoned from the documented design, not a claim of the Go documentation]:** (1) scan cost and pacing — gigabytes of pointer-free weights/KV on-heap inflate `HeapInuse` and distort pacing toward collections that can reclaim almost nothing; (2) span/class mismatch — the largest class is 32 KiB; a 4 GiB tensor is thousands of arenas' worth of extent governed by program-heap heuristics; (3) release semantics — a runner needs allocate → copy to device → release *now*, deterministically, against a hard budget; the heap offers release when reachability, sweep, and scavenger eventually agree; (4) the proven pattern exists — big, long-lived, pointer-free memory via `mmap` or pinned allocations, managed by a purpose-built pool (slab for KV, arena for weights, ring for staging), with only small descriptors (handles, block tables, metadata) on the Go heap, which is also how llama.cpp's buffers already live on the C side of Ollama's cgo boundary. The honest caveat travels with it: off-heap memory is invisible to `GOMEMLIMIT` and heap profiles, so the runner *must* re-implement that accounting in its pool statistics or it has traded GC pressure for operational blindness. The resulting routing table (allocator dossier §7.1) is this book's prescription for any Go runner: weights → off-heap mmap arena per model, released whole on unload; device KV → slab block pool with block table; host KV spill → same pool design over mmap; per-request/per-step scratch → per-request arena, reset at request end; server glue → Go heap, unmodified. And what *not* to do: do not interpose a C allocator under the Go runtime (its heap does not use `malloc`; interposition would affect only cgo code); do not put KV on the heap as per-sequence byte slices (span pinning plus GC pacing); do not tune `GOGC`/`GOMEMLIMIT` to manage device memory they cannot see.

Until one lands and wins under the campaigns' own discipline, the honest grade stands: **a correct, well-measured research allocator (v0.1.0) that loses on speed and wins on giving memory back.**

**What it did not prove** (carried owed, in spirit verbatim): no stable/production release; no Aesir comparison run (the Phase 12 Qwen3 comparison stayed OWED — model absent on the Pi, no aarch64 Aesir engine at the time; Z13's KV probes are harness shapes, not an integration proof — and they lost); no default global interposition claim, ever (opt-in only). **Lessons that transfer** (MimirAlloc dossier §5, distilled): do not expect a custom host allocator to speed up decode — decode is bandwidth-bound, not malloc-bound, and any allocator proposal carries the burden of a K-002-style A/B on the target machine; where an allocator *can* help a long-running server is footprint and longevity — measure RSS/PSS retained-after-free, not just throughput; page size and mapping policy are first-order on small machines; **free is where the tail lives** — measure alloc and free as separate distributions, always; optimisation level is part of the claim, in the same sentence as any factor, forever; and keep the owed list public and priced — it is the reason the proven parts can be trusted.

## Chapter 6 — Unified Memory: One Pool, New Failure Species

*This chapter synthesises `memory/unified-memory.md` — a synthesis dossier with a first-hand case study — with `runners/mlx.md`. The living case is Volmarr's ASUS ROG Flow Z13 (GZ302EA), machine name purpleraider: AMD Ryzen AI Max+ 395 ("Strix Halo"), Radeon 8060S iGPU (gfx1151), 128 GB unified LPDDR5X, Kubuntu. It is not an illustration chosen for convenience; it is the machine on which our fleet learned, in one morning, what unified memory does to a system budgeted as if it were two machines.*

### 6.1 What unified memory actually changes

A unified memory architecture (UMA) puts CPU, GPU, and usually NPU on one SoC attached to **one physical pool of DRAM**. No separate graphics memory, no PCIe bus between "system RAM" and "VRAM," no copy step when work moves between processors. Four moves follow (unified-memory dossier §1):

1. **Capacity stops binding — at consumer scale, for the first time.** "Does it fit?" is answered against most of the machine's RAM. Models that need four discrete cards fit whole. The headline is real.
2. **Bandwidth becomes the binding constraint instead.** Once the model fits, tokens/sec ≈ sustained bandwidth ÷ resident weight bytes. UMA memory is LPDDR, not GDDR or HBM: generous for an integrated part, modest next to a discrete card. UMA trades speed for capacity, deliberately.
3. **Zero-copy removes a tax and a cliff.** No host→device transfer, no duplicate residency during load, no offload cliff. Placement — which *processor* runs an operation — becomes a scheduling decision on shared buffers, not a data-movement decision.
4. **Allocation policy becomes the safety system — because the failure mode changes species.** On a discrete system, exhausting VRAM fails *locally*: the allocator errors, the process dies, the desktop lives. On UMA there is no protected island: weights, KV, compositor, browser, and kernel draw on the same gigabytes, and the GPU's share is typically *pinned* — unswappable and, in the worst documented cases, not reclaimable by the OOM killer. Exhaustion is not an application error; it is a **system-wide OOM that takes the desktop with it**.

> On unified memory, capacity is abundant, bandwidth is the price, and the budget is the safety system. A runner that does not budget explicitly has not removed the VRAM wall — it has moved the wall to the whole machine. *(Unified-memory dossier §1.)*

### 6.2 The architecture: mapped, not partitioned

Strix Halo combines 16 Zen 5 cores, a 40-CU RDNA 3.5 iGPU, and an XDNA 2 NPU on a **256-bit LPDDR5X-8000** interface, maximum 128 GB. The bandwidth arithmetic is elementary and every downstream number derives from it: 8,000 MT/s × 32 bytes = **256 GB/s theoretical**; secondary coverage consistently reports roughly **215 GB/s measured** in practice. The unified dossier is explicit about provenance: no AMD primary source publishing a sustained figure was found, and *our records contain no STREAM-class bandwidth measurement for purpleraider* — 215 is *reported*, 256 is *theoretical*, plan against the lower number, and the measurement is still owed (Chapter 10). The bus is wide for an APU (a dual-channel DDR5 desktop reaches roughly a third of it) and narrow for a GPU (an RTX 4090-class card exceeds 1 TB/s from GDDR6X) — precisely the decision that makes large-model inference *possible* on an integrated part, and precisely why it is *slow* relative to discrete hardware of similar compute. Memory is soldered: the 128 GB is a fixed endowment.

AMD's own ROCm documentation for RDNA 3.5 states the mapping model plainly (the dossier calls it the most important public paragraph in its evidence): RDNA 3.5 APUs handle memory through **GPU Virtual Memory (GPUVM)** — per-process GPU virtual address spaces — *rather than a separate, discrete VRAM pool*. Memory is **mapped rather than physically partitioned**. **GART** bounds platform address space mappable for kernel-driver use (small, mostly driver-internal). **GTT** (Graphics Translation Table) bounds system RAM mappable into GPU virtual address spaces *for user processes* — the pool PyTorch, ROCm, and LLM workloads actually allocate from; dynamic, not permanently reserved, reclaimable in principle when the GPU is idle; **default limit ≈ 50% of total system RAM**. A firmware/BIOS "VRAM" carve-out, by contrast, *is* a physical partition, and AMD's guidance is explicit that on physically shared memory it "provides little benefit for most workloads while permanently reducing available system memory" — keep the BIOS reservation small (their example: 0.5 GB) and size the flexible GTT/TTM pool instead. There is no performance distinction of the discrete kind: GTT-backed memory is not slower memory, because it is the *same* memory. In the dossier's words: **the GPU does not own a smaller, faster room inside the house. It holds a key that lets it map pages of the one house into its own address book, up to a configurable limit.**

On Linux the allocation path runs through **TTM** (the DRM bookkeeper for buffer objects, lifetime, placement, mappings). Two knobs govern, and they are set as a matched pair: `ttm.pages_limit` — the pool's page budget, exposed at `/sys/module/ttm/parameters/pages_limit`, **in 4 KiB pages, not bytes** (the single most error-prone unit in this whole area: 100 GiB = 102,400 MiB = **26,214,400 pages**) — and `amdgpu.gttsize`, the driver's GTT aperture **in MiB**. A mismatch means the smaller governs and the larger is theatre. AMD's supported front door is `amd-ttm` (in `amd-debug-tools`), which converts pages ↔ GB and writes the setting persistently; its worked example, `--set 100`, produces exactly 26,214,400 pages. AMD's clustering playbooks add the operational rule: **leave roughly 20% of system RAM for the operating system**. And the gap where §6.3 happened: AMD says GTT allocations are dynamic and reclaimable *in principle*; in practice, memory actively mapped and pinned for GPU work cannot be paged out and cannot satisfy the OOM killer — a deployment note in our stability research puts it bluntly: on a UMA machine, amdgpu GTT can pin system RAM the OOM killer cannot reclaim. **Swap does not rescue pinned memory** — our record's answer to "why is it not using the swap?" (a ~125 GB swap partition sat mostly beside the point while pinned memory OOM-killed the desktop; the Linux memory-redesign goal exists precisely because of this).

### 6.3 The Z13 case, told from our records

Every fact here is cited in the source dossier to the record it was read from; dates are EST as recorded. Purchase context: ordered 2026-10-29, received 2026-11-03, Walmart, $3,500, through a family member's Walmart account (so any return runs through that order).

Solo, attended, one-tenant workloads on the sprawl configuration ran clean. The 124 GiB setting was not stupid; it was a capacity decision correct for the workload it was tested under, and fatal for a different one. Two quieter data from the same week show how easily the pool gets claimed: a single Aesir test process creating a HIP `DeviceContext` **pinned +106.4 GiB of GTT for the suite's life**, flaking a downstream budget test by construction; and an Ollama tuning note — "Memory is plentiful on this UMA box (121.5 GiB GTT)" — while deciding a ~0.9 GiB KV saving bought nothing. **2026-11-14 — the cascade.** That morning purpleraider carried maximum multi-tenant contention: Aesir's 27 teams, Voxta, Ollama with large models, Chrome, and Yrsa's suites, on stock Linux memory settings plus the 124 GiB GPU pool. In the record's order:

On UMA, "it runs on the GPU" is a claim about an address mapping, not about speed.

**The economics (§6.1's arithmetic).** The 40B as Ollama held it resident: 44.2 GB — 35% of 128 GB, 44% of the capped 100 GiB pool; it fits with the 27B (~28.6 GB) beside it (~72.8 GB together), a combination Volmarr himself walked through when questioning the cap. **The commons.** On UMA the KV cache competes in the same pool: weights (fixed) + KV (grows with context × sessions) + prefix/snapshot stores (~158.9 MB per hybrid snapshot, bounded at 4 per session) + OS + desktop + every other tenant — **one sum, one limit, no internal walls unless someone builds them.** The failure at the limit is not "context truncated"; it is the 06:37 pattern — the kernel chooses a victim, and pinned GPU memory being unkillable, the victim tends to be the desktop, the session manager, and the user's other work. Long context is therefore not a feature to maximise but a *standing reservation against the OS*, made in the same ledger as the weights. **The inversion of the 6 GB problem:** the discrete card enforces the budget physically (failure = clean refusal at load, victim = the asking process); the UMA machine requires the budget to be enforced by the software the card made unnecessary (failure = system-wide OOM under load, hours in, victim = bystanders). Runners written for discrete GPUs treat host RAM as the forgiving overflow tier; ported naively to UMA, that habit allocates from the same pool twice in its accounting and once in reality.

**Standing fences now in force** (from the records): every heavy run fenced via `systemd-run` with named MemoryHigh/MemoryMax (24G/32G standard; the 27B serve under a larger, named 48G/64G fence); check MemAvailable first, stand down under ~40 GB free, heavy runs serialised; the fresh-boot law; pause discipline (all purpleraider work paused at Volmarr's word until his resume phrase; GUI proofs on a separate headless desktop, never his live session); and the redesign commission — live shields first (oomd tuning, cgroup fences, desktop protection), then a kernel patch series for unified-memory handling, published to the Z13 fixes repo and donated upstream with his sign-off. Volmarr's own design direction for it is recorded: on a unified device, prefer pageable paths where a stack allows, add a compressed in-RAM tier (zswap) ahead of disk swap, and make reclaim wake early and gently instead of escalating to the killer.

### 6.4 Apple and AMD agree; MLX teaches the vocabulary

Apple Silicon is the same bet taken earlier and integrated more tightly: one pool of in-package LPDDR; in MLX, arrays have no location — operations have a device (`runners/mlx.md`). The instructive comparison is bandwidth per capacity (Apple-published figures as carried in the sources read; unified dossier §2.3): M4 — 32 GB, 120 GB/s, 3.75 GB/s per GB; M4 Pro — 64 GB, 273 GB/s, 4.27; M4 Max — 128 GB, 546 GB/s, 4.27; Strix Halo — 128 GB, 256 GB/s theoretical (~215 reported), 2.0 (1.68 sustained). **At equal capacity (128 GB), an M4 Max offers roughly 2.1× Strix Halo's theoretical bandwidth** — which is why "both have 128 GB unified" is a deeply misleading equivalence. Capacity is the marketing number; bandwidth per capacity is the performance number. (Bandwidth-per-watt: the dossier asserts the direction of Apple's lead only and marks the magnitude unmeasured — no comparable figures could be grounded in the sources read. This volume carries that grading.) The other UMA designs confirm the pattern: Intel Lunar Lake (on-package LPDDR5X-8533, up to 32 GB, ~136 GB/s) is bandwidth-respectable and capacity-poor; Qualcomm Snapdragon X Elite (up to 64 GB, ~135 GB/s) is mid-capacity. **Everyone sells capacity; bandwidth is what they actually ration.**

Where the designs **agree**: one pool, zero-copy, capacity as headline, bandwidth as decode ceiling; and — independently discovered on both platforms — *the GPU may not have everything*: macOS caps the practical GPU working set at roughly 65–75% of physical RAM (Metal's recommended working-set discipline), AMD's default GTT is ~50% of RAM. Residency is active, not automatic, on both: MLX/macOS expose *wired* (pinned) memory controls, and MLX-LM documents raising the wired limit as the remedy for large models — pinning discipline reappears the moment copies disappear. Where they **diverge**: integration of the limit (Apple's is an OS/runtime contract the framework reads programmatically — `max_recommended_working_set_size` — and enforces in-process; AMD's on Linux is a kernel parameter set at boot and changed by reboot — same policy idea, very different ergonomics); bandwidth headroom; and ecosystem gravity (MLX designed *from* unified memory; ROCm designed for discrete accelerators and adapted to APUs — the seams show exactly where this chapter's pathologies live).

**The MLX budget-vocabulary lesson.** MLX's dossier distinguishes *four* distinct limits where naive accounting sees one "memory" number — the cache of freed buffers the framework retains, the wired limit, the recommended working set, and physical RAM — and the IDEA-LEDGER extracts the transferable parts as #31 (**one residency arbiter + ordered pressure shedding**: disaggregate the budget into named sub-ledgers — weights / KV / prefix cache / workspace / OS headroom as a first-class named reservation — under one arbiter, so failure becomes "prefix evicted," not "machine died") and #23/#35's capability declarations. The ledger also records the rejected translation (R14): MLX's "keeping a cache is cheaper than recomputing it" is priced in unified memory, where a dormant cache costs only its bytes and never left the pool; on a 6 GB discrete card, resident KV is priced against the weights themselves, and retention breadth must scale with the pool. **Vocabulary transfers; instincts must be re-priced per machine.** A single global cap hides the fight until it becomes a crash; named limits with one arbiter are the whole lesson.

### 6.5 Failure modes and pathologies — the field guide

From the unified dossier §5, as a checklist for any UMA deployment:

1. **GTT sprawl.** Raising the mapping limit toward total RAM converts every GPU allocation bug, leak, and arena over-size into a system event. Canonical exhibits: parameters requesting 124 GiB; one HIP context pinning +106.4 GiB as a side effect; a session arena reading as ~the whole pool consumed. None required a malicious or even buggy workload — only defaults sized to the pool instead of to the need. Countermeasure: explicit caps at every layer (kernel GTT, cgroup fence, per-session arena), each smaller than the layer above it.
2. **The "GPU memory is just RAM" budgeting fallacy.** GTT memory is the same DRAM, so it is tempting to budget it as ordinary process memory — overcommittable, swappable, reclaimable, forgiving. Pinned, it is none of those. The fallacy's tell: any sentence quoting *total* RAM as the budget for a GPU workload. The correct budget is always a named sub-ledger.
3. **Page-fault storms on first touch.** Mapped-not-partitioned memory must still be mapped: page tables built, pages faulted in. First-touch costs surface as load-time and first-token stalls — our sibling campaign caught the same physics from the CPU side, discarding a 56.7 tok/s "cold" reading that was really mmap page-in against a ~1,010 tok/s warm prompt-eval baseline. On the GPU side, long-context prefill can additionally trip device-timeout machinery (Ollama long-prompt `ErrorDeviceLost` at defaults on this platform class; owners raising `amdgpu.lockup_timeout` because long prefills exceed the ~10 s default watchdog, per the stability research). Treat first touch as a budgeted phase — pre-faulting, warm-up, watchdog awareness — not free setup.
4. **Coherence and synchronisation costs.** Zero-copy is not zero-cost: cross-engine work pays fences and cache maintenance, and concurrent CPU+GPU execution *shares the bus* — at decode, where the GPU alone can saturate bandwidth, splitting work across units adds nothing (the FusionML result reported in the MLX dossier: splitting helped prefill ~1.15–1.38×, did not help decode). Designs that "use everything at once" on UMA must show their bandwidth arithmetic, or they are spending the same 256 GB/s twice on paper.
5. **Invisible accounting.** Pinned/GTT memory is poorly represented in standard tools (`free`, RSS, most dashboards) — the redesign campaign's diagnosis names this directly. A system can report comfortable headroom while the un-reclaimable plurality is already committed. Any UMA health check without a GTT-specific readout (`mem_info_gtt_total` and friends) is flying on instruments that do not cover the risk. Our fix campaign's daily self-check already includes "GTT pool size" as one of its 16 checks — keep that pattern.
6. **Contention collapse at the desktop.** The compositor is a GPU client too; under memory pressure the display path starves with everything else, and the symptom ("the desktop froze") is systematically misread as a desktop bug. Triage rule from our morning: on a UMA machine under AI load, *every* desktop failure is a memory-budget suspect until the logs acquit it.
7. **Fresh-boot fragility.** A just-booted UMA machine is at its most deceptive: MemAvailable reads enormous, daemons and caches are still faulting in, shell components are at their crash-prone cold state. **[Speculation, flagged in the source: part of the mechanism is likely first-touch fault storms arriving simultaneously from every tenant; the record establishes the correlation and the law, not the full mechanism.]**

**UMA budgeting law, distilled [synthesis of the dossier's §6 rules]:** detect UMA and budget as one pool with named sub-ledgers and an OS reservation; default GPU-pool sizing conservatively and let raising it be a deliberate, logged act (the exact inversion of the community-chorus recipe our machine followed in good faith); load in resident format under a measured-fit rule, fenced; size KV from the ledger, quoting context as "X tokens at Y precision within budget Z"; ship the fences with the runner; and treat backend honesty on APUs (silent CPU fallback; ROCm BlockAllocator class versus Vulkan) as a memory-architecture decision, not a packaging detail. For MimirAlloc's line: on UMA the large-allocation path is not an edge case — weights, KV blocks, and session arenas are *all* large allocations — and a mapping-per-allocation strategy pays page-table and fault costs at exactly the scale the runner can least afford; arena/pool shapes with stated bounds are the UMA-appropriate form of its scoped wins, and return-to-OS policy stops being hygiene and becomes a safety property, because an allocator that retains freed arenas on a commons is a standing reduction of everyone else's headroom, invisible to standard accounting.

## Chapter 7 — Memory Above the Model: Agent Memory

*This chapter synthesises `memory/agent-memory-architectures.md` (OR-6 dossier A5). Its scope boundary is explicit in the source and kept here: the runner dossiers cover in-context memory — KV cache, prefix reuse, residency. This chapter covers the layer above them: the store that survives the end of a session, the machinery that decides what enters the next session's context, and the editor that is supposed to keep that store true.*

### 7.1 The two-layer truth

A deployed language model has two completely different things both called "memory," and confusing them is the root of most bad design in this area:

1. **In-context memory is a cache.** The context window, and the KV cache implementing it, hold a working set for the current computation: fast, exact, volatile. Its correctness problem is *capacity* — what to keep resident. Chapters 3–4 are its literature.
2. **Agent memory is a database with an editor.** Between sessions something must persist: facts about the user, a record of what happened, skills that worked, the persona that must not drift. That store is written by an *extraction* step (usually another model call), read by a *retrieval* step (usually embedding search plus ranking), and maintained — or not — by an *update* step. Its correctness problem is not capacity. It is **truth over time**: facts change, extractions hallucinate, retrievals return the stale copy instead of the current one, and nothing in a plain vector store notices.

> In-context memory is a cache; agent memory is a database with an editor, and most failures live in the editor. *(Agent-memory dossier §1.)*

A system can have a perfect KV cache and still lie to its user every morning, because the lie was written into the persistent store three weeks ago, was never superseded, and is retrieved faithfully. Conversely, a modest window fed by an honest store — versioned facts, visible provenance, a user who can inspect and delete — feels more trustworthy than a million-token window fed by a rotting store, because the window only amplifies what the store gives it. Three consequences organise the chapter: **writes matter more than reads** (the failures users report — "it remembered something I never said," "it still thinks I live in my old city," "it merged two different people" — are almost all write-side or update-side failures; retrieval faithfully surfaced a corrupt record); **every persistent memory system is therefore an editorial system** (designs differ mainly in who edits, when, and whether the edit is visible); and **append-only memory plus retrieval guarantees eventual contradiction** (§7.5). For Volmarr's commission the mapping is direct: character memory fails in exactly the editor's three classes — *loss* (the anchor falls out of context or store), *rot* (a superseded fact keeps being served as current), and *invention* (an extraction or reflection writes something never said). The runner can solve none of them.

### 7.2 The taxonomy and the operations

The agent literature borrowed its vocabulary from cognitive science and mostly kept the meanings. CoALA (Sumers et al., TMLR 2024) divides memory into *working* memory for the current decision cycle plus three long-term stores; Zhang et al. (2024) survey the *mechanisms* — how memory is written, managed, and read. Together: CoALA supplies the types, Zhang et al. the operations.

| Type | Holds | Concrete mechanism | Characteristic failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Working** | Active state for the current decision cycle | The context window plus structured state re-injected each turn | Overflow eviction; silent truncation |
| **Episodic** | Concrete experiences, anchored in time | Conversation logs / recall stores; Generative Agents' memory stream; Zep/Graphiti *episodes* kept verbatim as provenance | Retrieval miss; volume growth making ranking, not storage, the bottleneck |
| **Semantic** | De-contextualised facts abstracted from episodes | Extracted fact stores; profile summaries; entity–relation edges | Staleness and contradiction: two values for one slot, no rule for which is current |
| **Procedural** | How to do things: skills, plans, standing instructions | Voyager's verified skill library; system prompts and standing files; (in CoALA, also weights as *implicit* procedural memory) | A stale procedure that still executes |

Two distinctions product marketing routinely blurs. **Episodic is not semantic, and the step between them is lossy by design:** an episode says what was said; a semantic fact says what is *so*; getting from one to the other requires an inference that can drop a qualifier ("might move to Berlin" → "lives in Berlin"), merge two entities, or invent a connective detail. Systems keeping the raw episode beside the derived fact can audit the derivation; systems keeping only the derived fact cannot. *Provenance retention is not a logging nicety; it is the precondition for correction.* And **procedural memory a deployment can actually govern lives outside the model** — Voyager (Wang et al., 2023) is the canonical demonstration: a Minecraft agent whose skill library stores every *verified* JavaScript skill, entering procedural memory only after execution confirms it worked. Most conversational systems have no equivalent gate for semantic memory — a fact enters because an extractor produced it, not because anything checked it. That asymmetry explains a large share of memory hallucination in production.

Every store implements, explicitly or by accident, three operations (Zhang et al.'s write–manage–read loop): **Write** (extraction/encoding — verbatim retention, atomic facts, rolling summary, self-authored notes, reflection products; every honesty failure traces to a write that was wrong, over-confident, or unprovenanced); **Manage** (consolidation/update/forgetting — deduplicate, merge, supersede, expire, promote episodic → semantic; the least implemented operation in shipped systems and the most important for honesty); **Read** (retrieval/injection — select, rank by fused relevance/recency/importance/graph signals, budget, and place; the read step owns the *injection budget*). The dossier's diagnostic, worth memorising: **name a system's write, its manage, and its read. If it cannot name its manage step, it does not have one — it has accumulation.**

### 7.3 MemGPT/Letta: the OS metaphor made precise, and the anchor blocks

Packer et al., "MemGPT: Towards LLMs as Operating Systems" (arXiv:2310.08560, 2023–24), proposed *virtual context management*: treat the context window as an OS treats physical RAM — the fast, small tier of a hierarchy — and give the model system calls to page information between that tier and larger external tiers, with *interrupts* (a user message, a timer, a memory-pressure warning) handing control to the agent loop. On its Deep Memory Retrieval (DMR) evaluation, the reported lift over a fixed-context baseline is large (GPT-4: 32.1% → 92.5% as reported) — read with this corpus's discipline: DMR is the team's own benchmark over a specific dataset family with model-based judgement; it establishes that *managed* context beats *unmanaged* context on the authors' test by a wide margin, not a universal recall figure, and later cross-pipeline DMR comparisons are not commensurable.

Letta (the production framework, renamed from MemGPT in September 2024) refines the tiers into three: **core memory** (main context: a small set of labelled, editable *memory blocks*, always in the prompt), **recall memory** (the conversation history, searched on demand), **archival memory** (long-term facts and documents, inserted and searched via tools, typically over a vector index). The mechanics that make it an OS rather than a metaphor: function calls as system calls (memory operations are ordinary tool calls inside the agent loop); pressure signals as interrupts (at a warning threshold — ~70% of the window in the paper's worked example — the runtime tells the model to move what matters into persistent storage; at the flush threshold the oldest portion is evicted and replaced by a recursive summary — eviction *announced and summarisable*, in pointed contrast to silent truncation); and heartbeat continuation, because memory management takes multiple steps and the loop must let the agent spend steps on memory without confusing that with failing to answer.

The load-bearing primitive is the **memory block**: labelled, size-bounded, persistent, edited in place via append/replace tools. The bound matters as much as the editability — capped at hundreds to a few thousand characters, a block forces curation; to add a standing fact the agent must eventually compress, merge, or drop an old one, in the open, as a recorded tool call. The two conventional blocks are the point of contact with Volmarr's own law: the **`persona` block** (who the agent is) and the **`human` block** (who the user is) sit in *every* prompt, at a *stable position*, under a budget the block itself enforces. That is, in agent-layer form, exactly the **character-anchor pattern** this programme already enforces at the prompt layer (the character prompt at the end of memory in an unpushable anchor; resident context not reprocessed each turn; Chapter 3's IDEA-LEDGER #2/#7): *the small set of things that define who is speaking and who is being spoken to must be pinned, budgeted, and edited deliberately — never retrieved probabilistically. Retrieval is for the episodic and the archival. Identity is not a retrieval problem.* The converse is equally structural: anything not promoted into a core block is, by construction, allowed to be absent on any given turn — so a deployment must decide explicitly which facts are anchor-class and which are retrieval-class. Most consumer products make that decision implicitly and opaquely; Letta at least makes it a visible artefact.

Letta's later addition, **sleep-time compute**, moves the *manage* operation off the critical path: a second, background agent sharing the same blocks re-reads recent transcripts while the primary agent is idle, consolidates blocks near their limit, files material into archival storage, and — in the stronger descriptions — invalidates archival records newer material contradicts. The pattern generalises (ChatGPT's automatic updating is a closed version; Generative Agents' reflection is the research version): *consolidation is batch work; systems that do it inline make the user pay for it, and systems that never do it accumulate.* Letta's coding-agent line also versions memory as files under version control — a self-editing agent whose edits are diffs can be audited and rolled back; one whose edits silently overwrite a database row cannot.

The costs, stated honestly in the source: model calls spent on memory management (measurably slower and more token-hungry per turn — on a local deployment the memory manager competes for the same GPU and KV budget as the conversation itself); the editor is the model, with the model's failure modes (failed writes, wrong-substring replaces, concurrent-turn races on the same block, no built-in transactional lock); a floor on model capability (the paper's weaker small-model results were attributed largely to weaker function calling — directly relevant to small local models); and forgetting is not automatic (nothing fades unless an agent edits it out — safer for anchor-class material, worse for trivia). **Verdict on the lineage (source dossier):** MemGPT's contribution was not a bigger context but the reframing of context as a managed hierarchy with an accountable editor; Letta's was making the editor's workplace concrete. Every serious system since is a variation on how much of that editor the user is allowed to see.

### 7.4 Generative Agents: stream, score, reflection

Park et al., "Generative Agents" (UIST 2023; arXiv:2304.03442) — 25 LLM agents in a sandbox town over simulated days, evaluated by human judgements of *believability*, not production memory correctness; the framing must be kept. Its three components are the mechanisms every later system reuses:

- **Memory stream.** An append-only, timestamped, natural-language log of observations — with reflections and plans stored in the same stream and retrieved by the same machinery. One store, typed by content: episodic memory carrying its own derived semantic products — an elegant unification and a provenance hazard, since a reflection sits with the same standing as an observation unless the reader checks its type and cited evidence.
- **Retrieval score.** A weighted sum (equal weights in the implementation) of **recency** (exponential decay, factor 0.995 per simulated hour, over time since last *accessed* — retrieval refreshes recency, a rich-get-richer dynamic plausible for salience and dangerous for stale facts), **importance** (an integer 1–10 the model itself assigns at write time — a model judgement with all the calibration risk that implies), and **relevance** (embedding cosine similarity). Retrieval is *ranked, not filtered*: no truth gate, no supersession check. The score decides what the agent thinks with; nothing in it knows whether a memory is still true.
- **Reflection.** When summed importance of recent events crosses a threshold (150 in the implementation, firing ~2–3 times per simulated day), the agent generates salient questions about its recent memories, retrieves relevant memories per question, and writes inferences *citing the memories they were drawn from*. Those inferences re-enter the stream as a reflection tree — the most explicit published design for episodic → semantic consolidation in the literature covered.

The ablation (TrueSkill, human raters) ranked the full architecture highest (μ ≈ 29.89) ahead of no-reflection (≈26.88), no-reflection-or-planning (≈25.64), a human crowdworker baseline (≈22.95), and the full ablation (≈21.21); the two-day simulation produced the famous emergent party-diffusion results (a seeded intention spreading from 4% of agents knowing to 52%). What this establishes: each component contributes measurably to *perceived believability*, and a scored stream suffices to sustain coherent simulated persons over days. What it does not: factual precision of recall (a confidently wrong memory can be highly believable), behaviour at thousands of agents or months of growth, or cost viability (the authors report token costs in the thousands of dollars for 25 agents over two simulated days). Known honesty limits: retrieval still misses, silently; **reflection can fabricate** — invented conclusions enter the stream and are later retrieved with the same authority as observations (the citation requirement mitigates only for a reader who follows citations, which the acting agent mid-retrieval generally does not); and the store is append-only, ranked-not-filtered — corrections compete with the entries they correct under a score rewarding recent access. §7.5 returns to this as the general theorem; Generative Agents is its cleanest early exhibit, excused only because in two simulated days few facts have time to change.

### 7.5 Honesty and supersession: how memory stays true

*The source dossier separates published practice from engineering synthesis explicitly, and this section preserves the separation. Nothing in the synthesis is presented as industry standard.*

**The problem, stated exactly.** A fact in a user's life is a function of time — "lives in X," "works at Y," "prefers Z" each has a validity interval, a source, and a successor. A store recording the *statement* without the interval, source, and succession rule has stored a sentence once uttered near the fact. Supersession is the operation keeping the store's *current view* aligned with the latest truth while preserving the history explaining how it got there. Without it:

> **Append-only + retrieval ⇒ eventual contradiction.** Let any slot change value once. Append-only writing stores both values. Retrieval ranks by similarity/recency/importance — none a truth predicate — so for some future query the older value ranks in the injected set, possibly alone, possibly beside its successor (inviting the model to average, pick arbitrarily, or assert both). As changed slots and store age grow, contamination probability approaches certainty. No embedding model, reranker, or larger injection budget fixes this, because the defect is in the store's semantics, not the ranking. *(Agent-memory dossier §6.1.)*

**Published mechanisms** — four, not equivalent, making different promises:

- **(a) Model-decided update operations (Mem0 pattern).** At consolidation, a model compares each new extracted fact against retrieved neighbours and chooses add / update / delete / no-op. Promise: the current view usually wins. Limits: a model judgement with no transactional guarantee; a missed comparison (the old fact not among retrieved neighbours) leaves both alive; history is not, in the base design, a queryable audit trail. Supersession as *housekeeping*, not semantics. Mem0's transferable content is its boundary and vocabulary, not its scores: memory as *middleware* beside the agent with an explicit API; the four-outcome update decision as the minimal honest vocabulary (a store whose write path has only "add" has opted out of honesty by construction); and a bounded injection (thousands, not hundreds of thousands, of tokens). Its paper (ECAI 2025) reports 66.9% LLM-judged on LoCoMo against a full-context baseline at 72.9% — *lower* accuracy bought with ~91% lower p95 latency and >90% token savings, figures from the authors' own runs; later platform figures (LoCoMo ~92.5, LongMemEval ~94.4) are **vendor self-reports for a managed platform including proprietary optimisations, not peer-reviewed results**, and are not relied on beyond the lesson that the gap itself teaches benchmark non-comparability (§7.6).
- **(b) In-place self-edit (Letta pattern).** The stale substring inside a bounded core block is replaced. Promise: for anchor-class facts there is literally one place the fact lives in context, and editing it edits every future turn. Limits: archival/recall copies are not automatically reconciled — the same fact can be current in core and stale in archival, and a later search can re-import the stale version (a *resurrection* path). Versioned-file memory mitigates audit, not resurrection.
- **(c) Bi-temporal invalidation (Zep/Graphiti pattern) — the gold standard found.** Rasmussen et al. (arXiv:2501.13956, 2025): facts are edges between entity nodes in a temporal knowledge graph; each edge carries **two timelines — valid time** (when the fact was true in the world: `valid_at` → `invalid_at`) **and transaction time** (when the system learned it and stopped believing it). Raw inputs are preserved as *episodes*; every derived edge names its source episodes. Contradiction does **not** delete: the old edge's validity is closed and the new edge inserted. Promise: the graph answers both "what is true now?" and "what was true in March, and what did we believe in March?" — current view and history both first-class, supersession non-destructive and auditable. The paper reports 94.8% vs MemGPT's 93.4% on DMR and LongMemEval gains at far smaller injected context (authors' runs; §7.6 hygiene applies). Limits: extraction into entities/edges is still model work with model error; the machinery is the heaviest in this chapter; and bi-temporality answers "when was it true" while leaving "should the user have approved this write" untouched. **Of the four mechanisms, only Graphiti's makes the superseded fact remain queryable *as* superseded** — the only one fully satisfying the standard the Forge field map (A5) already states for memory work: *never silently drop; superseded/absorbed states are first-class, preserved states — not deletions wearing a friendlier name.*
- **(d) Automatic summary management (ChatGPT pattern).** The rolling summary is rewritten as context changes. Promise: no user labour; staleness attacked continuously. Limits: not versioned for the user, individual supersessions not attributable, drift uncheckable because prior versions and source episodes are not exposed. Supersession exists, but it is *unwitnessed*.

One line each, as the source gives them: Mem0 supersedes by *decision*, Letta by *edit*, Graphiti by *semantics*, ChatGPT by *rewrite*.

**Contradiction detection is retrieval-shaped, and that is its weakness.** Every published pipeline detects contradiction by first retrieving candidate conflicts, then judging the pair; if retrieval misses the conflicting record, no judgement ever runs. **[Synthesis, from the source dossier]** Slot-typed facts (a fact *about* an entity, *in* a named slot — residence, employer, preferred-name) can instead be checked *by key*, exhaustively and cheaply, at write time: a new residence value for a known entity *must* meet the old one, whatever their embeddings say. Free-text facts keep the similarity path; slotted facts get a guarantee. Most real-world staleness lives in a small set of slots — the guarantee is affordable exactly where it matters.

**Resurrection is the under-discussed failure.** Stale copies survive in the episodic log (correctly — episodes are history), old summaries not yet rewritten, archival inserts predating the correction, and the model's own later paraphrases ("as a Berlin resident, you…" — an episode that now *re-evidences* the stale fact for a future extractor). **[Synthesis]** The defence is directional provenance — explicit user statements about the present weighted above incidental presuppositions in old assistant prose — and a superseded fact's tombstone consulted at *read* time as a filter, not only at write time as a hope. No system surveyed documents a read-time tombstone filter; the source flags it as an open gap, not a solved problem, and so does this volume (Chapter 10).

**Abstention is a memory virtue, and it is benchmarked.** LongMemEval includes questions whose answer is *not in the history*, where correct behaviour is to say so. A system that always retrieves something and always answers fluently hallucinates precisely where its store is empty. **[Synthesis]** Injection should carry an explicit "no relevant memory found" state into the prompt, distinct from an empty injection, so the answering model can distinguish "we know nothing about this" from "memory was not consulted."

**Production patterns and their failure catalogue.** ChatGPT memory (OpenAI Help Center documentation as of 2026-11-15, behaviour level) separates three stores users routinely conflate: **saved memories** (discrete statements, stored *separately from chat history*, carried into future conversations, persisting until deleted — deleting the chat a memory came from does **not** delete the memory; full removal requires both), **reference chat history** (past conversations informing future answers without a discrete inspectable item; toggles asymmetrically with saved memories), and **custom instructions** (user-authored standing text — the control case showing what memory is *not*: memory is precisely the part the system writes itself). Controls: global on/off, per-item view/delete/clear-all, Temporary Chat (neither uses nor updates memory), and a disclosed retention note — deleted saved memories may be kept in logs up to 30 days for safety and debugging; "deletion" in the product sense means "no longer used for personalisation," with bounded back-end retention disclosed. An honest local system can do strictly better — deletion that is actually deletion — and should say so as a feature. The editorial evolution OpenAI's documentation itself narrates is the most instructive production honesty story in public: manual era (the list *went stale and contradicted itself*; users did not maintain it) → automatic management (updating, combining, prioritising in the background) → summary era (a continually updated broader summary, reviewable, with per-response source attribution; the legacy discrete list remains selectable). Read architecturally: the manage operation being staffed in three public steps, trading *inspectability for currency* at each. **[Judgement carried from the source]** For a character-memory system that trade is not close: a persona's facts must remain discrete, attributable, and individually deletable, even at the cost of more editorial labour. Summaries are for preferences; anchors are for identity. The production failure catalogue, each class traced to its operation: stale facts resurfacing (manage); memories the user never approved — extraction firing on a hypothetical, a joke, a quoted third party (write); qualifier loss (write); entity conflation — for character workloads the catastrophic case, persona facts and user facts cross-contaminating each other's stores (write/manage); deletion that does not delete (governance); summary drift — each re-summarisation slightly rewording the profile until it asserts things no conversation ever said, reflection fabrication at production scale with no citations to check (manage); injection surprise — a memory firing in an inappropriate context because relevance is topical similarity, not appropriateness, and no major public system documents an appropriateness gate (read). Production did not refute MemGPT or Generative Agents; it *repriced* them — and the discrete, inspectable memory list, the least glamorous artefact in this chapter, turned out to be the trust mechanism users actually use.

**The honest store [engineering synthesis in the source, assembled from the published mechanisms and Volmarr's standing laws; labelled synthesis throughout there and here]:** (1) **Versioned facts, not mutable rows** — each fact an immutable record `(subject, slot-or-text, value, valid_from, valid_to, recorded_at, superseded_by, source_episode_id, writer, confidence)`; "update" closes the old version and inserts the new pointing back at it (Graphiti's discipline restated as a storage rule any backend, SQLite included, can implement). (2) **Tombstones for deletion** — a deletion writes a tombstone (id, time, deleter) and removes the value from all serving views, *without retaining the deleted content*; tombstones exist to stop resurrection (a deleted fact whose episode still lives will be re-extracted unless something remembers it was *deliberately forgotten* — forgetting, in an honest system, is itself a remembered decision with the content removed), and a user-demanded hard purge must be able to destroy tombstone and provenance together. (3) **Provenance on every record** — source episode, writer (user-stated / extractor / reflection / sleep-time pass), and the exact injected set per turn; a fact that cannot name its source is downgraded — retrievable, flagged unverified, never promoted into an anchor block. (4) **User-visible memory as a right, not a settings page** — inspect (every current fact, with source and date), edit (a correction is a supersession, witnessed), delete (tombstone), export (the whole store in a portable plain format the user owns). (5) **Anchor separation** — persona/identity facts in a distinct, bounded, always-injected store with stricter write rules: writes require an explicit user statement or user approval; extractors and reflections may *propose* anchor changes but never commit them silently. *What must never be lost must never be silently rewritten either* — loss and corruption are the same failure wearing two coats. (6) **Approval tiers for writes** — user-stated "remember this" commits immediately; extractor-inferred durable facts commit visibly to a reviewable queue; extractor-inferred sensitive/identity facts are propose-only; reflection products commit as *derived* records, typed as such, citing evidence, never silently promoted to user-stated standing.

**The boundary argument for our builds [synthesis in the source, §7.1–7.2, carried as this book's prescription].** Stock Ollama is deliberately stateless — no server-side conversation object, history resent by the client every turn. Which layer owns the persistent store? **Not the runner:** it owns tensors, lives on a keep-alive timer, and is respawned precisely when configuration changes; coupling the most durable artefact in the system (memory, measured in years) to its most ephemeral process (a runner, measured in minutes) — and forking the store per model — is wrong. *The runner is the cache. Caches do not own databases.* **Not (only) the client:** right as a prototype, wrong as an end state — multiple clients would hold partial stores in unportable formats, repeating the deletion trap in miniature. **A memory service beside the runner:** a small, local, model-agnostic service owning the write–manage–read loop — versioned facts + episodic log + anchor blocks in a plain local database implementing the honest-store semantics; a write path running after a turn or in a sleep-time pass (with the caveat that a small local extractor is a *weaker* editor than a frontier model, so approval tiers matter *more* locally, not less; user-stated "remember this" bypasses extraction and commits verbatim); a read path returning a *budgeted, stably ordered, logged* injection set — anchor blocks first (always, pinned, fixed position for prefix-cache friendliness), then current-view semantic facts, then episodic excerpts, with an explicit empty state — behind a narrow local API (extract / search / add / supersede / forget / export) so any client shares one store, one editor policy, one audit trail. That is Mem0's middleware boundary with Graphiti's storage semantics and Letta's anchor blocks, assembled at personal scale; none of the three adopted wholesale. Two interactions the fork must handle: injection stability versus prefix reuse (volatile retrieved excerpts belong *after* the stable prefix, never interleaved into it — the same law as IDEA-LEDGER #14 and Aesir's HS-7 stable-order budgeted injection, the same shape at a different layer: *memory injection must be budgeted, stably ordered, and logged — the set injected on a turn is part of that turn's provenance; if you cannot list what was injected, you cannot debug what was answered*); and joint window budgeting (on a 4,096-token default window, generous injection and long conversation cannot both fit — the memory budget and `num_ctx` must be set with knowledge of each other, or truncation will silently eat whichever the template places worst — historically, the persona). For Aesir: its residency work is an unusually strong implementation of the working-memory tier plus anchor discipline — the in-context half, done at engine level. The other half is requirements, not yet design: a persistent store outside the engine process (engine residency a *cache of* the store's current view, never the store itself), an editor staffed as sleep-time background work writing *proposals* under approval tiers (inline self-editing would spend scarce decode budget on housekeeping), and a contract between the halves in which the store's read path *is* an HS-7 supplier with provenance carriage (each injected item arriving with fact-id and version, the session log recording the injected set). What Aesir should not do: grow memory features inside the inference engine itself, or adopt a rolling-summary profile as a character's long-term self-knowledge. And one honest line for MimirAlloc (source §7.3): **no real connection was found** — agent memory's problems are editorial, not allocational; the allocator enters only as the unremarkable substrate any local memory service's database runs on. This volume records that null result with the same prominence as its positive ones; a synthesis that invents connections its sources deny is committing the write-side failure this chapter exists to name.

### 7.6 Benchmarks that measure rot

Memory correctness finally has public benchmarks; two are verified in the corpus against their papers, and the surrounding vendor numbers require active distrust.

**LoCoMo** (Maharana et al., ACL 2024) — ten very long machine-generated persona-grounded conversations (~300 turns, ~9K tokens average, up to 35 sessions), 1,986 QA pairs in the public set, of which 1,540 non-adversarial questions are the conventionally reported subset (the fifth category is adversarial/abstention). The paper's finding stands: long-context and RAG systems improved over base models yet lagged humans substantially, worst on temporal reasoning. Caveats verified from secondary audits: the conversations fit inside a modern large context window, so LoCoMo no longer cleanly separates "has a memory system" from "has a big window"; an independent audit reported a non-trivial answer-key error rate (~6% in one 2026 audit — a single-team, non-peer-reviewed figure, quoted as a warning, not a constant); and scores are LLM-judge-dependent, so two vendors' LoCoMo numbers from different judges, subsets, and token budgets are not a leaderboard. Mem0's paper-run 66.9% and later platform claims above 90% are both "LoCoMo scores" and are not in contradiction — they are different pipelines wearing one name.

**LongMemEval** (Wu et al., ICLR 2025) — 500 questions, each in its own long multi-session history (S variant: ~115K tokens, ~40–60 sessions per question), testing five abilities: information extraction, multi-session reasoning, temporal reasoning, **knowledge updates**, and **abstention** (30 of 500). Headline finding: commercial assistants and long-context models drop ~30% in accuracy when memory must be sustained across interactions versus reading the evidence directly. For this book, LongMemEval is the more important benchmark because it is the only widely used one that *directly scores supersession and abstention* — the honesty operations — rather than recall alone. Its per-ability breakdown, not its headline number, is the honest way to read any system evaluated on it.

**DMR** exists and is reported by its papers (MemGPT 92.5%; Zep 94.8% vs 93.4%) but is not an independent community standard; small differences on it read as "comparable under the authors' pipelines," not as a ranking of ideas. **Explicitly not relied upon** (source §6.5): vendor leaderboards without judge/subset/budget disclosure; BEAM (encountered only via secondary/vendor summaries; its arXiv record was **not independently verified** for the dossier, so no BEAM claim is made); and any vendor-published "state of agent memory" survey, which is marketing literature even when its bibliography is real. The rule the dossier applied, recommended here verbatim: *a memory score is a claim until it names its answer model, its judge, its question subset, and its injected-token budget.*

**Section in one paragraph (source §6.6).** Truth has a time dimension and similarity does not. An append-only store treats "lived in X (2023)" and "lives in Y (2026)" as two equally real sentences; a retriever treats them as two equally eligible passages; an answering model, handed both or handed the wrong one, has no principled way to know one *replaced* the other — replacement was never recorded anywhere. Every component did its job. The system lied anyway. Honesty in agent memory is a *storage-semantics* property — versioning, invalidation, tombstones, provenance — purchased at write and manage time, and no improvement to embeddings, rerankers, or window size can substitute for it. The editor is the product. Everything else is plumbing.

## Chapter 8 — Memory Decisions Are Runner Decisions

*The cross-link chapter. Its method: take the runner corpus (`runners/`, `papers/`) and the synthesis ledger (`synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`) and restate their major decisions in memory vocabulary. The claim is not that scheduling, quantization, and speculation are "really" memory in some metaphorical sense. It is that each is priced, bounded, and usually decided by memory quantities — and that reading them through the memory lens predicts their behaviour better than reading them through the compute lens.*

### 8.1 Scheduling is admission control on memory

A serving scheduler's real question is never "which request runs next?" It is "whose KV may be resident, and whose must wait, spill, or recompute?" Every canonical scheduling invention in the corpus is a memory policy wearing a scheduling costume (the memory field map's founding sentence: memory is researched as a science in its own right "because most runner decisions are memory decisions wearing a scheduling costume"):

- **Orca's iteration-level scheduling** (`papers/orca.md`) admits and retires sequences at token granularity — which is to say, it re-decides *KV residency* at token granularity, keeping batch (hence decode intensity, §1.4) high without reserving max-length KV for sequences that finish early.
- **Continuous batching** (vLLM V1) is the same admission loop with a paged pool underneath: a sequence runs exactly while its next block can be satisfied. Pool exhaustion *is* the scheduler's back-pressure, and preemption-by-recompute (§3.8) is its eviction policy.
- **Chunked prefill and disaggregation** (Sarathi-Serve's stall-free batching; DistServe's prefill/decode split; Splitwise's phase splitting) exist because prefill and decode draw on *different* resources — compute versus bandwidth/capacity (§1.4). Disaggregation is, in memory terms, the decision to give each phase its own pool and its own admission rule, paying a KV transfer between them as the price of not letting a compute-bound tenant stall bandwidth-bound ones.
- **Cache-aware scheduling** (SGLang) orders waiting work so resident prefixes are used before they are evicted — the scheduler reading the cache's contents as part of its state, reaching ~96% of optimal hit rate in the RadixAttention paper's evaluation. Once the cache is content-addressed, scheduling *is* cache management.
- **Retraction** (SGLang's preemption) returns a victim's blocks to the tree as reusable cache — eviction designed to maximise the chance that "evicted" means "warm again soon," not "gone."

The local inversion matters as much: at batch 1 there is almost nothing to schedule, and the fleet machinery built for admission control (continuous batching as a throughput project, multi-process engine cores, disaggregation, tensor/pipeline parallelism) is **rejected for the single-user target** in the IDEA-LEDGER (R11) precisely because its memory wins scale with concurrency the target does not have. What transfers is the *shape*: an iteration-level step loop, a token-budget scheduler, block allocation. **[Synthesis judgement]** Judge any scheduling proposal by asking what it does to residency: if its author cannot answer in residency terms, the proposal has not yet met its real constraint.

### 8.2 Quantization is memory provisioning

Weight quantization is usually sold as a speed feature. Through the memory lens it is a *provisioning* decision — it decides how much model the machine can hold, and the speed follows from the provisioning via Chapter 1's ceiling (tokens/sec ≲ bandwidth ÷ resident bytes):

- On the Z13, Q8_0 → Q4_K_M for the 27B moved resident bytes ≈28.6 GB → 16.22 GB and measured generation 7.65 → 12.11 tok/s (1.58× against a 1.76× byte ratio) — a provisioning change whose speed consequence the roofline predicts and whose faithfulness consequence (Aesir's strict gate: DIVERGENT at 99.49% in-band) must be graded separately (Chapter 9). The same decision on the 6 GB card is starker: Q4-class weights are what make an 8B *admissible at all* beside a KV budget (§3.9).
- KV quantization (§3.5) is provisioning of the *other* resident object, with the asymmetry that its error compounds with context length while weight error does not.
- ExLlamaV2's EXL2 format and its quantised-cache evaluation work (`runners/exllamav2.md`), TensorRT-LLM's compiled, quantisation-first engine builds (`runners/tensorrt-llm.md`), LMDeploy/TurboMind's blocked-KV plus persistent-batch design (`runners/lmdeploy.md`), and llama.cpp's GGUF quantisation family (`runners/llamacpp-ggml.md`) are, in this reading, four provisioning dialects: each decides, per tensor class, how many bytes a unit of model quality costs, and each engine's fit-checker is a provisioning ledger. Aesir's compression school — teaching the engine to *write* the packed forms it already reads, graded packed-against-original — is provisioning brought in-house: the engine that can mint its own packed forms controls its own byte budget, at the price of owning the faithfulness grading (QS line: the Q8_0-resident configuration chosen for the live gate; the per-step dequant cautionary tale, QS-0 — dequantise outside the per-token loop, IDEA-LEDGER #25's mechanism, its exact ~35× figure carried in the Aesir dossier as briefed-but-not-verified and flagged in the ledger's Open Questions for confirmation against the on-machine record before outward quotation).
- PowerInfer (`runners/powerinfer.md`) is the provisioning lens applied to *placement*: activation sparsity means most neurons are cold for a given token, so hot neurons live on the GPU and cold neurons on the CPU — a residency policy over weights, worth ~1.5× for placement-over-random in its Falcon-40B ablation for the SwiGLU shape our catalogue actually has (IDEA-LEDGER #29), while its ReLU-family headlines (up to 11.69×) belong to models our catalogue does not ship and are rejected as a build target (R9). The free member of the family — gate-first threshold sparsity for SwiGLU (CATS, #30: ~50% MLP sparsity at ~1.5% quality cost, ~15% end-to-end in its setup) — survives precisely because it spends no resident bytes to save resident bytes.

### 8.3 Speculation is memory spent for latency

Speculative decoding trades the scarcest local resource — resident bytes and bandwidth — for the one thing batch-1 decode cannot otherwise buy: parallelism within a single stream. Read through the memory lens, the family sorts itself by *what memory it spends*:

- **N-gram / prompt-lookup speculation (IDEA-LEDGER #15)** spends *megabytes*: draft candidates from the prompt's own repetitions, no resident weights, lossless by construction (verification is exact). Bigram drafting alone measured 1.25× on T5-XXL in its source line. For repetitive character chat — the target workload — this is the default accelerator *because* its memory price is ~zero.
- **Draft-model speculation (#26)** spends *gigabytes of VRAM* — on a 6 GB card, byte-for-byte against the KV cache a long chat needs. Reported 1.5–2.6× for well-matched pairs (community, llama.cpp line) is real and conditional: the ledger keeps it as a per-pair, telemetry-gated option and **rejects it as the default** (R3) — a default that evicts context to fund a drafter is the wrong trade for this workload.
- **Trained draft heads (EAGLE line, #27; Medusa)** spend *hundreds of megabytes of heads* plus a training/artefact dependency: EAGLE-3's paper means of 3.45–4.44× (temperature 0–1, 8–13B targets, datacentre evaluation) are the family's best numbers and are worth nothing until a checkpoint exists for the exact model in hand; Medusa is deferred (R4) because community GGUFs ship without heads, and because its default *typical acceptance* rule does **not** preserve the output distribution — only rejection sampling is lossless, and any deployment promising "same output, faster" must use it and accept the lower speedup (`papers/medusa.md`). Embedded multi-token-prediction heads (#28) spend nothing extra *when the model ships them* — provisioning decided by the model author, harvested by the runner.
- **Lookahead decoding (full Jacobi form)** spends *FLOPs* — 56–120× extra per step in its tuned A100 configurations — absorbed only while decode is bandwidth-bound with compute to spare; its own RTX 3090 result (~1.3×) is the realistic consumer reference, and the ledger rejects it as an implementation target (R5) while capturing its pool-and-verify idea inside #15.
- **Session-granularity draft adaptation (#32)** spends *editorial* memory: per-persona drafters fitted to a session's distribution, acceptance +0.1 to +0.65 from distribution matching in the online-speculative-decoding line (cluster evaluation) — speculation beginning to draw on Chapter 7's store, which is where this family and agent memory meet.

**[Synthesis judgement]** The speculation literature's headline unit is speedup; its decision unit should be *bytes per unit of acceptance length*. Ranked that way for a memory-starved target, the family's order inverts its publicity order — the zero-byte member first, the artefact-dependent members last — which is exactly the ledger's ranking, arrived at independently. Two independent routes to the same ranking is what a robust conclusion looks like.

### 8.4 The ledger's top five, read through the memory lens

The IDEA-LEDGER ranks 36 ideas for the 6 GB single-user long-chat target (scored on expected gain for that workload, cost, risk — correctness risk first, since a silent quality loss outranks a crash — and evidence strength graded A/B/C per entry). Its top five, in the ledger's dependency order, are *all* memory decisions — the strongest single confirmation this chapter can offer:

1. **#2 Pinned, non-truncatable anchor segment + #6 Loud disclosure of every truncation, eviction, and reuse count** — scheduled first, together, as pure policy. In memory terms: an *eviction exemption* for the persona head, plus *accounting* (§2.2's fourth verb) made contractual. Neither needs new kernels; together they convert the build's founding law (the anchor cannot be pushed out) from an intention into a tested property, and install the observability every later slice is graded through. The only top-five work with no quality trade-off to validate.
2. **#1 Resident session object with token-level residency** — the object everything else attaches to: a server-side session owning its token stream and KV across turns, tracking exactly which tokens have KV committed (the committed-token watermark; generated tokens committed the moment they are produced; the final committed token always re-evaluated as the price of exactness). Per-turn prefill falls from O(history) to O(new tokens) *guaranteed*. Evidence A for the mechanism (Aesir gates, bit-exact records), C for the size of the win on the target card — the split grade is the ledger's honesty, preserved here.
3. **#3 q8_0 KV by default on small cards, K/V separate, per-model** — the arithmetic that decides what sessions can hold (§3.9: f16 affords 4,912 tokens, q8_0 affords 9,246, on the worked card). It must land before eviction scoring (#9) and before the persistence format freezes (#10), because both inherit the cache precision: scoring operates on the cache this entry sizes, and a saved session is valid only for the precision it was saved at.
4. **#4 Block (paged) KV allocation in one pre-sized arena** — the substrate: radix/hash retention (#5), block-granular eviction (#9), spill and persistence (#10/#11), per-layer quotas (#16/#21), and hybrid snapshots (#23) are all operations *on blocks*; without the arena + block table + refcounts, each would invent its own allocation shape and the campaign would pay five integrations instead of one. Its acceptance test is memory honesty — exact KV accounting, teardown returning provably to baseline (the L-006 lifecycle shape) — not speed.
5. **#5 Hash-chained / radix prefix cache: persona trunk + branches** — the retention layer, last of the five by dependency, first by felt impact. Once it lands, the workload's economics invert: later turns cost the new turn only, swipes cost a suffix, and tiering (#10/#11) has something worth tiering. The ledger's named dependency generalises: *prefix caching before tiering* — tiering is a policy over retained prefix objects, and scheduling it earlier produces a persistence format with nothing stable to persist.

What the five deliberately exclude is all speculation — it multiplies decode speed on a foundation that must first be correct, resident, and honest. The ledger's warning to a coordinator tempted to lead with EAGLE-3's headlines deserves quoting in spirit: those numbers are real, and they are worth nothing to a user whose persona was truncated at token 5.

The ledger's rejected list, read through the same lens, is a catalogue of memory mispricings: learned activation predictors rejected because their ~0.5B-parameter predictor is itself a resident-memory purchase (~0.5–1 GB) on a card with none to spare (R1); datacentre preemption-by-recompute rejected *as sole policy* because its price is denominated in the user's whole conversation (R2 — recompute as fallback survives inside #11); full KVQuant rejected as default because its calibration artefacts are a provisioning dependency arbitrary community models do not ship (R6 — its near-free steals, sink/head FP16 protection and 1% outlier sparing, extracted as #20); KIVI-2 deferred as an *unvalidated default* because its evidence caps at 4–8K contexts for most models while the target's entire purpose is long context (R7); non-prefix modular reuse deferred behind a quality gate (R8); a custom general allocator for the KV pool rejected *on our own measurements* (R10, Chapter 5); "infinite memory" readings of StreamingLLM rejected as a *claim* (R13 — sink+window gives stable generation over unbounded input and provides no recall of anything evicted; any UI copy letting "infinite chat" imply "remembers everything" is a dishonesty defect); and UMA retention breadth rejected as a wholesale import to a discrete card (R14, §6.4). **[Synthesis judgement]** A rejected-ideas list with prices attached is worth more than a roadmap: it records which trades were *considered and costed*, so the next engineer does not re-litigate them as novelties. Every research programme should publish one.

### 8.5 The remaining runners, in one memory paragraph each

**Ollama** (`runners/ollama.md`) is the patient: a Go control plane over llama-server (GGUF path) and an MLX runner (Apple safetensors path), slot-partitioned contiguous KV windows allocated up front (`num_ctx × num_parallel`), per-slot last-prompt-only exact-prefix reuse that dies on unload, no in-run eviction, and the two-layer silent truncation that Chapter 3 and the ledger's #2/#6 exist to abolish. Its memory story is the local end of every spectrum in this book — which is why the Forge operates on it: the distance between its current memory behaviour and the corpus's proven designs is the build campaign. **llama.cpp/ggml** (`runners/llamacpp-ggml.md`) is the gravity well: the cell-addressed unified KV pool (positions logical, not physical), per-layer K/V tensors, the RAM cache and slot files of §3.8, and the `llama_memory_i` abstraction that already knows memory is plural (§3.7). **vLLM** (`runners/vllm.md`) is PagedAttention productised — V1's block pool, hash-chained prefix caching on (`runners/sglang.md`) is reuse as an organising principle: radix tree, cache-aware scheduling, HiCache tiers with per-tier layouts, the Unified Radix Cache voting across FULL/SWA/MAMBA components, and ## Chapter 9 — Measurement Discipline: How Memory Claims Are Proven

*Memory is the easiest quantity in computing to misreport and the hardest to fake once the reporting rules are fixed. This chapter collects the corpus's epistemics — from STREAM to the MimirAlloc protocol gates to Aesir's oracle grading — into one discipline. It is the chapter to hold every other chapter's numbers against, including this book's own.*

### 9.1 The four numbers, never one

Any honest allocator or memory report separates (allocator dossier §6.1):

1. **Requested / live** — sum of user-requested sizes currently live.
2. **Allocator-held** — live + internal fragmentation + free-but-cached/retained.
3. **Resident (RSS/PSS)** — physical pages actually backed; what the machine feels. PSS (proportional set size) is the honest variant under shared pages.
4. **Virtual / reserved** — address space obtained; cheap on 64-bit hosts, *the* budget on VRAM and UMA.

Fragmentation claims must say which gap they measure: (held − live)/live is overhead + internal fragmentation + retention; (RSS − live) adds page-granularity effects; **external fragmentation proper is only proven by a failed allocation that total-free says should have succeeded, or by reporting the largest free block alongside total free.** A report giving only RSS and live data has measured retention and called it fragmentation — the most common measurement error in the field, refused explicitly by the allocator dossier and by this book. The instruments exist and are named in the corpus: glibc `mallinfo2()` (main arena only — multi-arena programs need `malloc_info()`), jemalloc `mallctl` (the only allocator here whose statistics decompose all four numbers natively: allocated → active is internal fragmentation + metadata; active → mapped is retention awaiting decay; mapped → retained is virtual held after purge), tcmalloc's `MallocExtension` properties and sampling profiler, mimalloc's `mi_stats_print()` / `mi_process_info()`, Go's `runtime.MemStats` (`HeapAlloc / HeapInuse / HeapIdle / HeapReleased / HeapSys`) — plus, for any off-heap pool, *the pool's own counters*, there being no runtime shortcut — and on VRAM, the driver view versus the framework view (PyTorch `memory_summary()`: allocated vs reserved vs reserved-unallocated, the last being the fragmentation+retention gauge). Allocator overhead itself is a *distribution*, not a mean: per-op p50/p95/p99 for alloc and free *separately* (Chapter 5's lesson that the tail lives in free), with timer resolution stated and a p50 at the timer floor reported as "≤ floor," never as a precise value.

### 9.2 Sustained versus peak; roofs measured on the machine

STREAM's founding correction (§1.2) generalises into law: **vendor peaks are upper bounds on upper bounds.** The roofline dossier's protocol (§4.3 there) requires, for every benchmark: the machine's roofs *measured on the machine* (sustained bandwidth per memory tier in play — VRAM, host RAM, per-CCD where relevant; peak compute only if compute claims are made), with governor/clocks, contention, and co-tenants recorded — a wall measured under contention is not a baseline, and the Z13 logs void or flag contended runs for exactly this reason. The workload's bytes (model file size *and* active bytes — they differ under spill; KV bytes/token × context; batch; prompt length): without the byte count, a tok/s number cannot be converted into the only form that transfers between machines — **achieved bandwidth and % of sustained peak**. Phases separately (prefill and decode are different regimes with different roofs; a single blended tok/s over a short generation mostly measures whichever phase dominated the stopwatch). And distributions over repetitions — medians, spread shown, drift checks between identical legs — never single runs.

What a claimed tok/s does and does not prove (roofline dossier §4.3, carried verbatim in substance): it *does* prove that this binary, model build, context, batch, and machine state produced that rate; converted to implied bandwidth against a measured roof, it can prove a run is *at the wall* (nothing left without fewer bytes or more bandwidth) or *below the wall by a stated gap* (implementation headroom, sized by the gap). It does *not* prove the engine is "fast" in general; that a change *caused* an improvement (without an A/B under protocol, drift and contention are equally good explanations — cf. the Z-054 glibc leg that had to be DRIFT-REJECTed); that compute is bottleneck or saviour (at batch-1 decode it is neither); or anything about correctness, which tok/s does not measure. **Red flags that void a tok/s claim on sight:** batch/context/quant unstated; prompt-processing tokens folded into generation rate; comparisons across different byte counts presented as engine comparisons (a Q4 number vs an FP16 number is primarily a *bytes* comparison); peak vendor bandwidth used as the roof; a single run on a shared machine. The one-line discipline: ***never report a speed without its bytes, its phase, its machine state, and its distance from a measured roof.*** Our own fleet supplies the cautionary exhibits: the 56.7 tok/s "cold" Ollama reading discarded as mmap page-in against a ~1,010 tok/s warm baseline (§6.5); the stock-Ollama 100%-CPU run at 16.82 tok/s that was a placement defect, not a speed (§6.3); purpleraider's still-owed STREAM-class measurement, without which every bandwidth figure for that machine in this book is labelled reported or theoretical (§6.2).

### 9.3 The MimirAlloc protocol: identical legs, pre-registered bars, evidence packets

The two allocator campaigns practised the discipline this chapter preaches, and their protocol — not any single number — is the transferable asset (allocator dossier §6.3; MimirAlloc dossier §§3–4):

- **Identical-legs A/B.** One workload source compiled once; only the allocator function pointers differ; legs alternated across ≥5–7 fresh processes; medians reported; **per-workload checksums required identical across legs before any timing is believed** (K-002 protocol). The Z-004 protocol gate quantified the method itself: identical-leg noise ≤0.85% against a 5% bound, drift 2.28% against an 8% bound — with drifted attempts *voided and kept as evidence* (attempts 1–8 of one gate, preserved, not deleted).
- **Pre-registered adoption bars.** A variant that won its target traces was still rejected for regressing an oracle workload past a bar fixed *before* the run (Z-095, 2% bar, +6.13% regression, NO-ADOPTION). Regressions printed in full. NO-ADOPTION recorded as a normal verdict, repeatedly (Z-059, Z-112).
- **Separate distributions.** Alloc vs free, p50/p95/p99, timer floor declared (K-008: ≈37 ns).
- **Footprint as a first-class metric.** Retained-after-free measured against baseline, with the record's own qualifier carried (retention is reuse-ready, not a defect per se — but it is footprint a caller pays).
- **Host facts attached to every verdict.** Page size (16 KiB Pi vs 4 KiB Z13), build flavour (the `-O0` caveat INC-K002-02, discharged by later `-O2` runs), contention state, machine identity. *A factor without its host is not a result.*
- **Final-gate evidence packets.** Each campaign closed with a packet (M-009 Phase 13 Exit Evidence; the Z13 closure packet, 566 lines as banked) that grades every criterion — including the PARTIAL, OWED, and UNMET ones — beside the headline gate numbers (4,590/0; 4,988/0/77). The packet's function is adversarial to its own authors: it makes the strongest honest claim the *only* claim available, because the weaker grades are printed in the same document. **[Synthesis judgement]** This is the form every memory claim in production should take: a gate number, a grading table that includes the failures, and the raw runs preserved. A claim whose evidence packet cannot be produced on demand is marketing, whatever its author's intentions.

### 9.4 Raw-oracle grading: Aesir's contribution to the epistemics

Speed evidence answers "how fast"; it cannot answer "how faithful." Aesir's campaigns contribute the complementary discipline (`aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md` §6.2; IDEA-LEDGER #34), born of hard lessons the AGENTS record preserves (raw-oracle discipline: never judge an engine against a template-rendering comparator — a different input; grade with teacher-forcing rank + logit gap on the raw prompt; record the oracle backend per row; CPU engine against CPU comparator only):

- **The faithfulness band.** Near-tie = rank ≤2 with sub-logit gap = faithful. Token-exact parity is *not* demanded where the underlying maths does not provide it (ggml's own CPU vs CUDA IQ1_S kernels disagree with each other — grade coherence, never demand token-exactness the reference itself lacks).
- **Comparator artifacts are a verdict class.** Template rendering, BOS rendering, backend mismatch, and prompt-boundary step-0 effects each fake divergences present in neither engine's maths. Check the comparator before suspecting the engine.
- **Every divergence gets a written verdict class** or a named road with re-open criteria — no unexplained rows. The compression school's grades show the vocabulary working: a Q4_K_M product admitted by all four readers, graded DIVERGENT at 99.49% in-band — a sentence that simultaneously ships the artefact and refuses to overclaim it.
- **Trust only disk.** Wave-era worker reports were fabricated more than once in the wider programme; every number from files/git objects/catalogue listings, every model sha256-pinned before running. The MimirAlloc keeper rule is the same law in another uniform: verification against `git show --stat` and gate logs before banking, never against a worker's summary.

Applied to memory work (ledger #34's translation): **every lossy change — KV precision tier, eviction policy, packed format — is graded against the full-precision/full-cache oracle at the longest supported context, per model, before the tier is labelled for that context.** Perplexity alone is an insufficient oracle for cache changes (KVQuant's retrieval results, §3.5, degrade far faster than its perplexity — grade retrieval too). And the roofline law (#33) binds the build itself: no tok/s claim without bytes, phase, context, and a measured roof. The two laws together — *grade faithfulness against an oracle, grade speed against a measured roof* — are this book's complete answer to "how do you know?"

### 9.5 Disclosure as measurement: the runtime half

Offline discipline has a runtime twin. A memory system that measures honestly in the lab but stays silent in production has moved the dishonesty, not removed it. The corpus's runtime disclosure requirements recur across dossiers with unusual unanimity: every response carries tokens reused vs evaluated, segments/tokens dropped by any truncation or eviction, and the effective window and cache precision in force (IDEA-LEDGER #6); the serve advertises which memory properties actually hold per session — Aesir's `exact_prefix_reuse` / `session_residency` / `memory_injection` capability pattern — truthfully or not at all; OOM and refusal messages name their cause in the four-number vocabulary (PyTorch's allocated/reserved/reserved-unallocated wording as the template; llama.cpp's single generic 400 for every slot-restore failure as the anti-template); and a memory service logs the exact injected set per turn as provenance (Chapter 7). **[Synthesis judgement]** Disclosure is not telemetry garnish. It is the mechanism by which every other chapter's discipline survives contact with users: a truncation nobody reports will be "fixed" by users blaming the model, a precision nobody surfaces will be validated for contexts it never ran, and a budget nobody itemises will be spent twice. The cheapest correctness feature in this entire book is a counter, printed.

## Chapter 10 — Gaps & Frontiers: An Honest Research Agenda

*A synthesis that hides its thin places teaches its reader to trust it uniformly — which is to say, falsely. This chapter maps the corpus's coverage against its own field map (`memory/00-MEMORY-FIELD-MAP.md`, topics A–F), names what is shallow and what would fill it, and then separates genuine frontiers (where the field itself has not arrived) from mere gaps (where the field has arrived and our corpus has not yet followed).*

### 10.1 Coverage audit against the field map

**Deep (multiple dossiers, first-hand evidence, or both).** A1 KV-cache design (the corpus's largest dossier); A2 KV compression & eviction (H2O, StreamingLLM, SnapKV, PyramidKV dossiers); A3 KV quantisation (KIVI, KVQuant dossiers); A4 context-window economics (derived in the KV and roofline dossiers, worked to a purchasing decision in §3.9); A5 agent memory (a full dossier, Chapter 7); A7 attention variants as memory trade-offs (GQA, MLA dossiers; §3.6); B5 the memory wall (a full dossier, Chapter 1); B7 unified memory (a full dossier with a first-hand case, Chapter 6); E1 allocator lineages, E2 specialised shapes, E3 fragmentation theory, E5 managed vs manual (one deep dossier, Chapter 5); E6 MimirAlloc (a full first-hand dossier); F1 measurement methodology (practised across campaigns, Chapter 9); F2 memory in serving systems (vLLM/SGLang dossiers, §§3.8, 8.1); C1 VRAM budgeting and C5 device fragmentation (worked cases throughout); A9 deployed memory and the anchor law (Chapters 7–8).

**Present but thinner (a mechanism dossiered, its neighbourhood not).** A6 recurrent/SSM state as memory — deep for the Qwen3.5/Aesir case and SGLang's generalisation (§3.7), thin on the wider hybrid catalogue and on state *compression* (an open research question, not a gap of diligence). C2 access patterns (coalescing, bank conflicts, GEMV kernel craft) — present inside the FlashAttention and Aesir dossiers, not treated as a subject in itself. C3 on-chip memory — likewise, present via FlashAttention/MLX, not systematised. D4 Hailo as the public case — present via Aesir campaign records mined as our own work; the public dataflow literature (D1–D3: SRAM-centric design, weight/output/row-stationary dataflows in the Eyeriss lineage) is *named* in the field map but has no banked dossier. A8 memory-augmented lineage (Neural Turing Machines, DNC, retrieval-as-external-memory, memory layers) — named, not dossiered; RAG is covered operationally via CacheBlend/Prompt Cache, its ancestors are not.

**Shallow — the B-side, stated plainly.** The commissioning brief for this volume names the asymmetry and this chapter confirms it from the inside: **B-side depth — the computer-science of memory itself — is thinner than the A/E sides.** Specifically:

- **B1 Virtual memory & paging** — page tables, TLB structure and shootdowns, demand paging, thrashing, page replacement, huge pages as a *system* topic. The corpus touches paging constantly (PagedAttention borrows its metaphor; TTM manages mappings; huge pages appear as allocator tactics in jemalloc hooks and MimirAlloc's THP slices) but has no dossier on the OS machinery itself. *What would fill it:* a B1 dossier on Linux memory management as it bears on runners — page-table walk costs at 128 GB scale, TLB reach under multi-GB resident sets, THP collapse behaviour under pinned GTT, shootdown costs in many-core serve processes — measured, where possible, on our own fleet (the Z13 Linux memory-redesign campaign is already commissioned to produce much of this evidence; its outputs should be banked as the B1 dossier, not left as a side quest).
- **B2 Cache hierarchies** — L1/L2/LLC organisation, associativity, replacement policies, prefetching, cache-oblivious design, false sharing. False sharing is covered as an allocator pathology (Hoard); the hierarchy itself is not. *What would fill it:* a B2 dossier anchored in measurements the corpus already half-owns (Strix Halo per-CCD L3 and cross-CCD latency, MimirAlloc Z-011/Z-012) extended with working-set sweeps for decode kernels — the level at which "fits in L3" becomes a design variable for small models and KV tiles.
- **B3 NUMA & topology** — first-touch placement, interleaving, cross-socket costs. Present as Z13 CCD facts and an ARENA_PER_CCD decision filed in the MimirAlloc campaign; absent as a general treatment (dual-socket serving, CXL-attached NUMA — see B8). *What would fill it:* a topology dossier generalising the CCD record, plus first-touch policy for mmap weight arenas (Chapter 5's routing table currently says "huge-page-eligible" without a placement policy — an honest incompleteness this chapter owns).
- **B6 DRAM generations** — DDR / LPDDR / GDDR / HBM compared on bandwidth, capacity, power, packaging, and fleet presence. Present as scattered anchors (Strix Halo LPDDR5X-8000 arithmetic; Apple bandwidth ladder; A100/H100 HBM figures) but never as the comparative dossier the field map promises. *What would fill it:* a B6 dossier with per-generation sustained-bandwidth-per-watt and per-dollar tables, HBM3e/HBM4 and LPDDR6 roadmaps from vendor sources, and a fleet mapping (which of Volmarr's machines carries which generation, and what that predicts before any benchmark is run).
- **B8 CXL & pooling** — memory disaggregation, pooled tiers, memory as a fabric resource. **Absent** beyond the field map's one line. *What would fill it:* a B8 dossier on CXL 2.0/3.x pooling as published (device types, fabric topologies, measured latency adders over local DRAM from the literature), graded for runner relevance: pooled memory as a KV tier (§10.2) is the application that would justify the dossier.
- **C4 Host↔device paths and C6 CUDA/HIP memory models** — pinned memory, zero-copy over PCIe and unified fabrics, UVA, migration costs; the two programming models' memory semantics as publicly documented. Present in fragments (UMA dossier; PyTorch allocator notes; Aesir HIP lessons including the BlockAllocator failure class) but not systematised. *What would fill it:* a C4/C6 dossier pair, with the HIP-on-APU path documented from our own records first (the fleet's scarcest knowledge is the knowledge only our fleet has generated).
- **NPU dataflows beyond the Hailo public case (D1–D3)** — as above: the field map promises Eyeriss-lineage dataflow analysis; the corpus delivers Hailo campaign records and SRAM-centric reasoning inside other dossiers. *What would fill it:* a D-side dossier on dataflows as memory policy (reuse distance as the primary energy/latency lever), with Hailo as the worked public case and the general theory stated once, properly.
- **GPU-memory internals** — driver segment management below the framework allocator, graphics/compressed-memory paths on APUs, page-table formats. The corpus documents behaviour at the TTM/GTT boundary (§6.2) and stops there, honestly. Vendor documentation thins at exactly this boundary for all three vendors; a dossier would be part literature, part black-box measurement, and should say which parts are which.

**Process gaps (not topics, but evidence debts named in the dossiers themselves).** purpleraider's sustained bandwidth never STREAM-measured (§6.2 — every Z13 bandwidth figure in this book is therefore reported or theoretical, and the December verdict work should not proceed on unmeasured roofs); the qwen35 attention interval unfixed in the record (§3.7 — one GGUF metadata read settles it); the §3.9 planning reserve (512 MiB) unmeasured on its target card; Z-003's factor-to-workload mapping unverified from its recorded run (§5.5); MimirAlloc's line counts resting on a study record (§5.5); the Aesir QS-0 per-step dequant factor briefed-but-not-verified (§8.2); block size for a single-sequence pool disputed inside the corpus (§3.3 — settle by sweep, not citation); and the IDEA-LEDGER's inherited Ollama-line unknowns (engine routing per model family at the pinned version; whether measured-placement scheduling survived the return to llama-server; MLX-path prefix reuse and truncation behaviour — all recorded as not fully verified in `runners/ollama.md`, to be settled by direct checks against the build actually forked). A research programme is known by whether its debts are listed where its claims are. They are listed here.

### 10.2 Frontiers — where the field itself has not arrived

- **Tiered KV to SSD as a first-class tier.** The pieces exist (HiCache L3 backends; LMCache; CacheBlend's NVMe-priced fusion; llama.cpp slot files at 2.14 s restore) but the *policy* science does not: which prefixes earn SSD residency, at what precision, with what prefetch discipline, graded by restore-time-versus-recompute on consumer NVMe rather than datacentre fabrics. The local case is the most promising and least published: a single user's persona trunk and branch history on a fast local SSD is a different problem from a fleet's shared cache, and almost certainly an easier one. Frontier grade: engineering frontier, not science frontier — buildable now, unbuilt in the corpus's local runners.
- **CXL-attached pools.** When memory becomes a fabric resource, Chapter 2's ladder gains a level with near-DRAM bandwidth at multiples of local latency, and every verb in §2.2 must be re-priced for it: KV tiers pooled across machines, weights shared at fabric scope, failure domains that include "the pool left." The corpus has no evidence here at all (B8 above); the honest statement is that CXL pooling will first matter to this programme as *someone else's* serving infrastructure, and the design habit that prepares for it is the one this book already teaches — never hard-code which level a byte class belongs to; let the ledger decide.
- **Memory-centric architectures.** The wall's logical terminus: processing-in/near-memory, SRAM-centric NPU designs where the working set must live on-chip (field map D1), and hybrid bonding/stacked-memory parts that collapse the ladder's middle levels. The corpus's evidence is one-sided (Hailo records; Strix Halo as a halfway house — capacity of a memory-centric part, bandwidth of a conventional one). The open question for the next edition: whether "the model is the memory" designs (weights resident in the compute fabric, state never leaving the package) change the runner's job from *managing* memory to merely *addressing* it — and what allocation means when there is nothing left to allocate.
- **State compression for recurrent memory.** §3.7's flagged unknown: KV can be evicted per-token because it is token-addressable; a fused recurrent state cannot. Whether a state can be approximately summarised, factorised, or checkpointed at multiple fidelities — a precision ladder for *state* analogous to §3.5's ladder for cache — is a genuine research frontier with direct product consequences (branching and persistence costs on hybrids are denominated in whole states). No corpus source demonstrates it; asserting it as a capability today would be speculation, and this book does not.
- **Read-time honesty machinery for agent memory.** §7.5's flagged gap: tombstone filters consulted at read time, directional provenance weighting, appropriateness gates on retrieval. The write/manage side of honesty has a gold standard (bi-temporal invalidation); the read side has benchmarks that measure its absence (LongMemEval abstention) and no published mechanism that closes it. Frontier grade: the most consequential open problem in Chapter 7's domain, and the one most amenable to a personal-scale system proving the pattern first.
- **Learned versus principled memory policies.** Eviction scoring, snapshot placement (which prefix boundaries earn a 159 MB checkpoint?), and injection budgeting are currently principled heuristics with measured constants. Whether learned policies beat them *under oracle grading at production contexts* — rather than on the short-context suites where most KV-compression evidence lives — is unresolved in the corpus's sources and is the empirical question the next research wave should be designed around. The corpus's own discipline supplies the method: pre-registered bars, identical legs, oracle grading, no adoption on a loss.
- **Accounting standards.** Chapter 9's four numbers have no cross-vendor standard form: every framework's "memory" output decomposes differently, UMA pinned memory is invisible to standard tooling (§6.5), and agent-memory scores arrive without their budgets (§7.6). A modest, unglamorous frontier: a common reporting vocabulary — requested/held/resident/reserved, plus largest-free and pinned-invisible lines — adopted widely enough that claims become comparable by construction. The Forge's own dossiers already write to that standard informally; formalising it is cheap and would outlast most kernel work.

### 10.3 The agenda, in order
